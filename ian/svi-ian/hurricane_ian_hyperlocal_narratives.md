## 1. Cape Coral — the land-sale scheme underneath the map

**This is the sharpest and most underused thread in the whole build.** Cape Coral got a fraction of Fort Myers Beach/Sanibel's media coverage despite comparable or worse aggregate damage, and the reason is structural: it wasn't a dramatic single-image collapse (a severed causeway, a beached shrimp fleet) — it was diffuse, inland, canal-adjacent flooding across a city built explicitly to look like waterfront paradise from a sales brochure.

**Origin story.** Cape Coral was built by Gulf American Land Corporation (GALC), founded 1957 by brothers Leonard and Jack Rosen — former mail-order shampoo/"baldness cure" hucksters from Baltimore, financed by Chicago's Jay Pritzker. GALC bought 103 sq. mi. of mangrove swamp for $678,000 in 1957 and began dredging in January 1958. By the late 1960s it was the largest land-sales company in the US — 25% of all Florida land sales, 372,276 acres nationally — before its 1969 sale for $200M+.

**The sales machine.** GALC ran its own airline (Gulf American Airlines, five DC-3s) plus a contracted carrier, flying up to 500 prospective buyers/day free from the Northeast and Midwest into Fort Myers for a "free flights, free meal, free sales pitch" tour. Salespeople were told to "take 'em — if we don't, someone else will." Visitor clubhouse rooms were reportedly bugged to overhear buyers' private conversations. Lots were deliberately oversold as a hedge against contract defaults.

**The fraud crackdown.** Florida Gov. Claude Kirk (1967 campaign line: "the era of 'let the buyer beware' is over") empowered a reconstituted Florida Land Sales Board to investigate. The American Stock Exchange suspended Gulf American stock trading; the company pled guilty to five counts of fraud in November 1967, was fined, and put under court-ordered monitoring. Named lawsuits: two Massachusetts corporations sued for $4.14M alleging misrepresentation; a Detroit couple sued after being told their lot was "2½ city blocks from the Gulf" when it was actually 9 miles away. (Ironic detail: three of the five Florida Land Sales Board members had Gulf American ties at the time.) St. Petersburg Times editor Elizabeth Whitney coined the term "swamp peddlers" in her Pulitzer-winning 1970 investigative series — later the title of Jason Vuic's definitive 2021 history, *The Swamp Peddlers* (UNC Press).

**The design flaw, in engineering terms.** The technique was "dredge-and-fill": material dredged from riverbeds/swamp bottoms was piled up to raise finger-canal lots above the 3.5-foot legal minimum, with the removed material carved into the canals themselves — trading wetland storm-surge buffering for "waterfront" lots. Stephen Strader (Villanova, disaster geography): *"You have a natural wetland marsh... the primary function of those regions is to protect the inland areas from things like storm surge. You're building on top of it, you're replacing it with subdivisions and homes. What do we expect to see?"* And on the canal-lot sales logic: *"It's just the same reason why golf courses have lots of water hazards... now everybody's got a waterfront property — but it also means you get more water intrusion."* Michael Grunwald (Politico, 2017) called Cape Coral "a boomtown that shouldn't exist." Only three hurricanes made direct landfall on this stretch of coast between 1960 and Ian — a 60+ year "false calm" during which local sea level (Fort Myers gauge) rose ~8 inches, letting the canal-grid model expand essentially untested until 2022.

**Scale today:** 400+ miles of canals — the highest concentration of waterfront residential lots of any city in the world.

**How Ian actually hit it.** 99% of Cape Coral lost power; every traffic light was dark, forcing 4-way-stop conditions at major intersections (National Guard/police directing traffic at Pine Island Rd/Del Prado Blvd). Wind gusts to 140 mph; 6–8 inches of rain. Ian pushed several feet of water into inland, canal-adjacent neighborhoods — not just the coastline. One Cape Coral native described her family holding their back doors shut against wind pressure for 13 hours straight. First Street Foundation ranks Cape Coral #1 in the nation for flood-risk properties: 116,216 properties (89.5% of the city) at risk of flooding within 30 years; Lee County ranks #1 nationally among counties for 100-year-flood-exposed properties.

**The comparative-neglect story, on the record.** Cape Coral residents have said so directly (Sarasota Herald-Tribune, via Yahoo): Jenny Morales (Coral Lakes): *"I feel like Cape Coral is not getting the necessary attention to the needs of its citizens... The services that were promised have been sent to other places."* She contrasted it to a 2020 Connecticut tornado where she had power back in 5 days. Heather Walsh: *"We need answers more than our stories shared. Where is FEMA?... I saw a post from Sarasota County and they have tons of distribution sites set up. We have nothing."* The article's own framing attributes the imbalance to media focus on visually dramatic single-site destruction (Sanibel Causeway, Fort Myers Beach) over Cape Coral's diffuse, less-photogenic inland flooding.

**Post-Ian institutional fallout mirrors Fort Myers Beach exactly:** FEMA moved to strip Cape Coral's NFIP flood-insurance community discount over improper post-Ian rebuilding, which would have raised premiums ~25% immediately; the city held hearings for 200+ flagged homeowners. One resident, Sherry Oakes, already paying $8,000/year, was flagged despite only minor storm damage. Mayor John Gunter called the FEMA move "another catastrophic event."

**Tool implication:** Cape Coral should carry its own scored "development-era" dimension distinct from Fort Myers Beach's insurance-collapse narrative — pre-1993 (pre-NFIP-participation) canal lots vs. later-code construction, layered under the same Insurance Market Stress axis but with its own explanatory alert card: *"Built as a mass-market land-sale product, not a luxury coastal destination — got less coverage, comparable damage."*

*Caveat: one AI-synthesized secondary source claimed "southeast Cape, built pre-1993, saw multiple feet of standing water for a week" — flagged as needing primary-source verification before treating as fact in the tool; use only as a hypothesis to test against parcel-level data.*

## 2. Recent transplants who didn't know the risk

- **Jerry Smith** — moved to Cape Coral from New Jersey during COVID (2021) specifically for canal frontage, pool, boat dock, direct Gulf access. Minor Ian damage, but now facing insurance-rate shock: *"The insurance industry has been tough on people here... I think it's one of the burdens that people have to really contemplate. Can they afford it?"* (NPR, Greg Allen)
- **Steve Zielinski** — moved from Philadelphia roughly three years before Ian; rode out the storm with 8 people, 2 dogs, 4 cats in a 900-sq-ft home. Afterward: *"Next time, if it's going to be a 2 or more — we're out of here."*
- **Robert Reecer** — a newcomer whose lanai flooded overnight; stuffed towels under doors, grabbed an axe, moved his family to the attic: *"Being in the attic hearing wind blowing 120 miles per hour and trying to get in contact with family I thought I may never see again was the worst part."*
- Realtor Sam Yaffe, on Cape Coral's pandemic-era appeal versus pricier coastal markets: *"People started discovering us... Our prices are cheaper."*

**The structural information gap behind this (directly relevant, statewide):** Florida was one of roughly 18–21 US states with no legal requirement that sellers disclose flood history to buyers, until a new law took effect October 1, 2024. Pre-2024, disclosure obligations rested on a 1985 Florida Supreme Court standard requiring disclosure only of facts "materially affecting value... not readily observable and not known to the buyer" — enforceable only through expensive after-the-fact lawsuits. FEMA itself keeps "repetitive loss" and "severe repetitive loss" property addresses confidential — the one entity with the best flood-history data withholds exactly what a buyer would need (Miami Herald/WLRN, "Floods of trouble," Alex Harris). The real-estate industry — the single largest donor bloc to the FL legislature ($86M in 2024; 66 of 159 lawmakers had real-estate ties in 2023) — resisted disclosure mandates for years before recently reversing position. Named cases from that investigation (statewide, not Lee/Collier-specific, but establishes the mechanism directly applicable to any SW Florida buyer): Samantha Fischetti (Hollywood, FL — flooded 3 ft inside three months after purchase, repair costs since exceeded the $340K purchase price); Jesse Carl Smith IV (St. Petersburg — told "slight flooding," later learned of five prior floods and 9 inches of interior water); Ashley Bowman (Fort Lauderdale — a neighbor told her on move-in day the house was known on the block as "the flood house," despite a 4/10 First Street Flood Factor rating).

**Tool implication:** a tract-level "buyer information asymmetry" framing note in the Insurance/Adaptive-Capacity tab — even where FEMA/NFIP data exists, it is often structurally hidden from the people who'd most need it before buying.

## 3. Serial rebuilders — Charley (2004) → Irma (2017) → Ian (2022)

**Punta Gorda / Charlotte County is the clean case study.** Hurricane Charley put its Cat 4 core directly over Punta Gorda on Aug. 13, 2004 (~$16B statewide damage, ~1M without power). Post-Charley rebuilding used the tightened Florida Building Code (further updated 2007).

- **Joe Schortz**, Punta Gorda contractor: *"Charley was almost like a spring cleaning event... Charley destroyed a lot of the older homes with the winds."* On Ian, 18 years later: *"Everything with a 2007 code and beyond pretty much was fine"* — but *"quite a few places did get ravaged,"* largely structures that survived Charley without subsequent upgrade. (Washington Post, Oct. 1, 2022)
- **Charlotte High School** — rebuilt after Charley, survived Ian with barely any damage. It carries a plaque: *"This school has risen from the rubble to reawaken as the magnificent, enduring structure you see today. Never again will the winds be feared, never again."* — a striking, quietly ironic artifact given the school faced a second major hurricane 18 years later.
- **Monty's restaurant**, owner Diane Caputo — destroyed by Charley (took 3+ years to reopen); performed "much better" in Ian at similar storm strength.
- **Parkhill Estates**, a 176-home 55+ community in Punta Gorda — newer housing stock "mostly withstood" Ian's winds.
- Expert framing: Nicholas Rajkovich (SUNY Buffalo architecture/planning): *"It's a demonstration that updated building codes really work... Buildings built to newer codes consistently have fared better."* Kathy Baughman McLeod (Adrienne Arsht-Rockefeller Foundation Resilience Center), who lived through both Andrew and Charley in FL, calls Andrew the "game-changing hurricane" that triggered Florida's building-code turning point.
- **Quantified building-science data (IBHS):** homes built after 1996 (SW Florida code tightening) showed 40% fewer damage incidents and 60% lower damage severity in Charley; post-2002 Florida Building Code homes showed fewer roof failures/envelope breaches and markedly lower total-loss rates in later storms including Ian. (Primary source: ibhs.org/wind/building-performance-in-sw-florida-during-hurricane-ian-2022/ — worth pulling directly for tract-adjacent structure-age scoring.)

**Tool implication:** this is the direct evidence base for weighting a building-code-era field (pre-1996 / 1996–2002 / post-2002 Florida Building Code) as a scored structural-sensitivity variable — not just a narrative aside, an actual field with IBHS-backed differential damage rates.

## 4. Babcock Ranch — the resilience counter-example

Inland Charlotte County, ~30 ft above sea level, built from 2015 onward by developer Syd Kitson (former Green Bay Packer): underground power and fiber lines, engineered wetlands for stormwater retention, all homes built above code, 700,000 solar panels (only a handful damaged) paired with 10 MW of battery storage. Result: **zero loss of power, water, or internet during Ian**, while more than 90% of surrounding Charlotte County lost power.

- Kitson, at Aspen Ideas: Climate: *"I'm sitting in my house, and it was like this perpetual freight train running right through us and it just seemed like it was never gone to end."* On the wetlands: *"They're gorgeous... but they also provide this massive amount of surface water storage that was incredibly helpful."*
- Land-use frame: Kitson bought the 91,000-acre ranch in 2006, sold 73,000 acres to the state for conservation (part of the Florida Wildlife Corridor) as part of the development deal — Tom Hoctor (UF Center for Landscape Conservation Planning) frames it as concentrated/inland development preserving 75–80% of surrounding land, an explicit design contrast to Cape Coral/Golden Gate Estates-style sprawl-and-drain.
- Resident account: Christina Schuler (WINK News producer, Babcock Ranch resident since 2020) didn't board windows or need a generator; worst damage was broken screen doors and missing shingles.

**Honest counter-framing (this is thin in existing coverage — treat as your own synthesis, not a reported critique):** homes range from low $300,000s to $1M+; a potential buyer (Edward Reese) was priced out and bought elsewhere. Jane West (1000 Friends of Florida) concedes smaller developers "simply don't have the resources that Kitson was able to amass" — implying the model may not scale without comparable capital. A genuinely reported irony: despite demonstrated resilience, Babcock Ranch homeowners reportedly receive **no insurance discount** for it — insurers aren't pricing in the risk reduction their own engineering delivers.

**Tool implication:** strong Resilience-tab anchor — "what already works" — but pair it with the affordability/scalability caveat so it doesn't read as "just build it like this," which would undercut the tool's own argument about structural exclusion elsewhere.

## 5. Sanibel Causeway collapse

At least 3 (some sources say 5) causeway spans were washed out on Sept. 28, 2022, severing Sanibel/Captiva's only road link to the mainland. The islands had 6,400+ year-round residents; Sanibel's mayor said roughly 200 households did not evacuate. Immediate rescues: 12 people rescued with injuries, ~40 without, reachable only by boat or helicopter once the causeway broke. FDOT restored emergency access almost immediately, resident travel access within 30 days, and fully rebuilt the causeway (storm-hardened design) by Christmas 2023 — an unusually fast infrastructure turnaround.

**Adjacent, less-covered isolation story:** Matlacha (historic fishing/artist village) and Pine Island were also cut off — sections of the Pine Island bridge roadway washed away, isolating Bokeelia and St. James City residents for days, resupplied by boat/helicopter. At least one Matlacha-specific death has been reported. As of 2026, Matlacha's community center/art building and the Little Pine Island Bridge have been rebuilt, marking the village's 100th anniversary.

## 6. Elderly / assisted-living specifics (beyond the general power-outage pattern)

- Evacuation scale: ~47 nursing homes and 115 assisted-living facilities evacuated roughly 8,000 residents ahead of the storm; a separate count puts it at 40+ nursing homes evacuating ~3,400 residents, mostly to affiliated facilities outside the storm path. Lee County officials were criticized for delaying evacuation orders until Tuesday for a Wednesday landfall.
- **Orange Harbor Mobile Home and RV Park** (Fort Myers Shores, 55+, 365 trailers) — ~90% of homes damaged or destroyed when the Caloosahatchee River flooded the park; black mud contamination ruined flooring and electrical systems, and the park's water/sanitary systems were compromised. A strong, specific, nameable elderly-community casualty case with real numbers, distinct from Poinsettia.
- **Shell Point Retirement Community**, Fort Myers — Florida's largest single-site continuing-care retirement community (~2,500 residents, 1,200+ employees). Residents sheltered in two purpose-built on-site shelters. Storm surge dumped five feet of water on parts of the complex, forcing evacuation of 250 units; damage estimated up to $85M ($30–35M direct out-of-pocket to the community). A reported family-negligence complaint against Shell Point exists (WINK News) — worth pulling directly if a specific evacuation-failure allegation is needed for a detail-panel alert card.
- Parkhill Estates (Punta Gorda, 55+, 176 homes) again serves as the resilience-side counterpoint here too.

## 7. Insurance non-renewal granularity

No source publishes clean zip-code-level non-renewal or claims data for Lee/Collier. The more interesting finding is that this is **structurally hidden by design**, not merely unpublished: FEMA redacts specific flooded/repetitive-loss addresses from public release, and a homeowner can only see their own property's claim history after they already own it and already carry an NFIP policy. Statewide baseline for scale: Florida's homeowner non-renewal rate rose 280% from 2018–2023, reaching 2.99% in 2023 — the steepest rise in the nation.

**Tool implication:** frame the tool itself as partially compensating for this — tract-level SVI/NRI/insurance-proxy mapping fills a gap regulators and insurers keep opaque by design, which is a legitimate meta-point for the "why this tool" framing.
