Hazard
What physical risk exists?
The Almeda Fire started near Ashland on Sept. 8, 2020 and, driven by 40–45 mph wind gusts and severe drought, ran ~15 miles north along the Bear Creek Greenway/Highway 99 corridor through Talent and Phoenix into south Medford in a matter of hours. Fire spread is the dominant, wind-driven hazard; structure density (wildland-urban interface exposure) is scored as a secondary factor.

Failure Modes
What breaks when a fire like Almeda hits?
Almeda's exclusion mechanism is domestic, not informal: manufactured-home residents held full legal title to their houses, but not the land under them, on lots inside privately owned parks. This tab scores that Manufactured Housing Tenure track and the Emergency Alert Failure track — Jackson County issued no broadcast alert at all, and Talent's opt-in Everbridge system never fired — as their own combined "what broke" view, separate from the full Vulnerability composite. Documentation-status exclusion (undocumented/mixed-status farmworker households ineligible for FEMA aid) is the same mechanism already scored in the Vulnerability tab — reused from Western NC and Ian, not reinvented here.
LowerHigher failure exposure
FEMA denied roughly 57% of the ~27,000 individual-assistance applications filed after Oregon's 2020 wildfires. For any household with an undocumented member, FEMA's Individuals and Households Program and Disaster Unemployment Assistance are categorically unavailable, full stop.
Resilience
What is already working?
Talent Mobile Estates — with CASA of Oregon's help — became the first resident-owned manufactured home community (ROC) in the Rogue Valley: 89 displaced households now govern the park itself, closing the tenure trap rather than just documenting it. New Spirit Village (Proud Ground/ACCESS, Medford) is shown as a reference marker, not part of the scored grid — a ~$28M, 87-home community land trust built specifically for Almeda Fire survivors, with permanent affordability built into the land itself.

Human Stories
What did it feel like to live it?

The Talent Mobile Estates resident who now owns the park

Nearly all 99 homes in the park burned. Now part of the Rogue Valley's first resident-owned manufactured home cooperative — the tenure trap not just documented but closed, through CASA of Oregon's ROC model.

The farmworker family FEMA can't see, again

A mixed-status household on a Bear Creek corridor mobile home lot, working Rogue Valley pear orchards or vineyards, lost everything but never appears in a FEMA registration count — the same trap as Western NC's Christmas-tree workers and Ian's Immokalee field labor, filled instead by UNETE and Coalición Fortaleza.

The Talent resident who got no warning at all

No Everbridge alert, no emergency broadcast — not a bureaucratic exclusion or documentation gap, but a literal absence of warning in a town the county's own after-action review found under-resourced and under-trained to protect.

The park that didn't come back

Not every one of the corridor's 18–19 destroyed mobile home parks has rebuilt at the same pace; some sit as vacant lots along Highway 99 five years on — a landlord's decision the residents who once lived there had no legal standing to contest.

The five-years-later return

A family that left the Rogue Valley after the fire, priced out or discouraged by the slow rebuild, set against a family now moving back into a rebuilt or resident-owned home. Talent's population has recovered to near pre-fire levels — but not household-by-household, and not evenly across tenure types.
