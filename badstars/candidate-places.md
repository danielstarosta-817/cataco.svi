# Candidate places for Raised Under Bad Stars

A research shortlist of places, disasters, and cultural/artistic responses not yet in the tool (39 cases currently: 30 full, 9 dimmed). Organized by region. Each entry notes a rough framework stage guess, but that's a starting point, not a verdict, since a few of these could argue for more than one stage. The strongest, most-developed candidates below get fuller treatment; several are flagged honestly as needing more digging before they're worth building into full cases, and are kept brief on purpose.

## Outline

**East & Southeast Asia, Pacific**
- Pompeii and Herculaneum, Italy (79 AD): the eruption that killed Pliny the Elder has generated an unbroken 2,000-year run of cultural output, from Pliny the Younger's eyewitness letters to a modern tourism industry.
- Mount Merapi, Java, Indonesia (ongoing risk): a Sultan-appointed spiritual gatekeeper tradition collided fatally with modern evacuation orders during the 2010 eruption.
- Tangshan, China (1976 earthquake): a death toll of 240,000+ was politically suppressed for decades before a 2010 blockbuster film reintroduced the disaster into public memory.
- Sri Lanka (2004 tsunami): the wave derailed one of the deadliest passenger trains in history, and the wreck site is now an informal pilgrimage stop.
- Mount Pinatubo, Philippines (1991 eruption): displaced the Aeta Indigenous community and altered global climate for a year, a potential full-case companion to the tool's existing Krakatoa entry.

**South Asia**
- Bhuj/Kutch, Gujarat, India (2001 earthquake): post-quake recovery funding revived a traditional embroidery economy into a major export industry.
- Bhola cyclone, Bangladesh (1970): still the deadliest tropical cyclone on record, it inspired the 1971 Concert for Bangladesh and led directly to a national cyclone-shelter network.
- Nepal (2015 Gorkha earthquake): Newar communities rebuilt damaged temples using inherited apprentice woodcarving techniques rather than outside restoration methods.

**Middle East & Central Asia**
- Ashgabat, Turkmenistan (1948 earthquake): the death toll was classified for 40 years under Soviet rule, with estimates now running as high as 176,000 dead.
- Turkey/Syria (2023 earthquake): too recent for a settled cultural record, but worth revisiting once more material accumulates.

**Europe**
- Messina, Italy (1908 earthquake): once the deadliest earthquake in European history, now overshadowed in memory by Lisbon and Pompeii.
- Skopje, North Macedonia (1963 earthquake): rebuilt with Cold War-era international solidarity aid, later complicated by a controversial 2014 architectural makeover.
- Aberfan, Wales (1966): a coal-waste tip collapse killed 116 children and 28 adults and remains an open wound in Welsh memory.
- Laki eruption, Iceland (1783-84): triggered a famine that killed roughly a fifth of Iceland's population and entered the country's saga-length literary memory.
- Venice, Italy (Acqua Alta and MOSE): centuries of flooding are baked into Venetian art and identity alongside a famously delayed engineering fix.
- Ireland, the Great Famine (1845-52): sustained largely through diaspora memorials and folk song rather than through the place itself.

**Africa**
- Sahel drought/famine cycles, West Africa: griot oral tradition carries drought memory forward generationally, covered in depth in the Africa document.
- Goma, DRC (Mount Nyiragongo, 1977/2002/2021): a city that has rebuilt directly on top of its own lava flows multiple times.

**Latin America & Caribbean**
- Armero, Colombia (1985 Nevado del Ruiz eruption): the photograph of Omayra Sánchez became one of the most reproduced disaster images in the world.
- Valdivia, Chile (1960 earthquake): the largest earthquake ever recorded, deeply embedded in national memory but needing more research on specific cultural responses.
- Managua, Nicaragua (1972 earthquake): corruption in the aid response is credited as a spark for the Sandinista revolution.
- Yungay, Peru (1970 earthquake and avalanche): an entire town of roughly 20,000 was buried in seconds and left undisturbed as a memorial.

**North America (not yet covered)**
- Dust Bowl, US Great Plains (1930s): Guthrie, Steinbeck, and Lange's photography make this the most culturally documented American disaster not yet in the tool.
- Galveston, Texas (1900 hurricane): still the deadliest US natural disaster by death toll, with a seawall that physically restructured the city around memory.
- Johnstown, Pennsylvania (1889 flood): a dam failure killed over 2,200 and entered folk song almost immediately.
- Peshtigo, Wisconsin (1871 fire): the deadliest US wildfire, almost entirely forgotten for sharing a night with the Great Chicago Fire.
- New Madrid earthquakes (1811-12): reportedly rang church bells in Boston and fed regional religious revivalism.
- Charleston, South Carolina (1886 earthquake): left visible "earthquake bolt" retrofits still recognized on historic buildings today.
- Halifax, Nova Scotia (1917 explosion): Boston's relief effort is repaid with an annual gift Christmas tree, a rare gratitude-driven tradition.
- Alaska (1964 Good Friday earthquake): the second-largest earthquake ever recorded, alongside Alutiiq/Chugach oral tsunami traditions older than the seismic record.

**Worth a research pass before deciding**
- Chernobyl/Pripyat, Ukraine (1986): enormous cultural footprint, but much of it external rather than the affected community's own.
- Bhopal, India (1984): rich activist and literary output, closer to industrial than natural-hazard disaster.
- Christchurch, New Zealand (2011): strong art memorialization via the "185 empty chairs" installation, worth a closer look.
- Assam, India: Brahmaputra flood cycles tied to festival timing, promising but thin so far.

## East & Southeast Asia, Pacific

### Pompeii and Herculaneum, Italy, 79 AD eruption of Vesuvius

This is arguably the most obvious gap in the tool, since Pliny the Elder is already quoted as the Formulation chapter epigraph and he died at Pompeii, killed while sailing toward the eruption to rescue people and observe it more closely. His nephew, Pliny the Younger, wrote letters to the historian Tacitus describing the eruption in detail, and those letters are considered the first eyewitness disaster account in Western literature, a foundational document for the entire idea of disaster testimony as its own genre.

The eruption has generated an unbroken 2,000-year run of cultural output since. Edward Bulwer-Lytton's 1834 novel *The Last Days of Pompeii* turned the disaster into popular fiction, and it has been repainted, restaged, and reproduced across countless works since. A modern tourism industry is now built entirely around the disaster site, with millions of visitors a year walking streets preserved by the same eruption that destroyed them.

Given Pliny the Elder's existing role as the tool's own epigraph, this case is strengthening, with an unusually long tail: almost no other disaster in this entire research project has two thousand continuous years of documented cultural response to draw from, making Pompeii a natural anchor case for arguments about how disaster memory persists across centuries rather than years.

### Mount Merapi, Java, Indonesia, ongoing volcanic risk

Merapi has a living spiritual-gatekeeper tradition: a *juru kunci*, appointed by the Sultan of Yogyakarta, who performs an annual offering ceremony and is believed to interpret the mountain's warnings directly. The role isn't ceremonial in a purely symbolic sense; the gatekeeper is treated by many nearby residents as a genuine authority on when the mountain is dangerous, standing alongside, and sometimes in tension with, government seismologists and evacuation planners.

That tension became fatal in 2010. The gatekeeper at the time of the eruption, Mbah Maridjan, died in his village trusting the traditional warning system over the government evacuation order. His death is a hard case precisely because it isn't a story of ignorance overridden by science; it's a story of a specific, named, socially legitimate authority choosing his own tradition's judgment over an official order, and paying for it with his life.

That makes Merapi a strong Formulation and Strengthening case, with a real Operationalizing-versus-tradition tension built into it: a modern seismic monitoring system exists alongside a centuries-old spiritual office, and the 2010 eruption is the clearest possible evidence that the two don't always agree on when the danger is real.

### Tangshan, China, 1976 earthquake

Death toll estimated at 240,000 or more, actively suppressed and downplayed for political reasons under Mao, whose government framed disaster relief as unnecessary given official self-reliance policy. That suppression meant a disaster of enormous scale was effectively pushed out of public memory for decades, a Degradation case driven directly by state censorship rather than by time or distance.

Decades later it resurfaced as the subject of *Aftershock* (2010), a Chinese blockbuster that effectively reintroduced the disaster into public memory on a national scale. A single film accomplishing what decades of silence had suppressed is a clean Degradation-then-revival arc, and it pairs naturally with the tool's existing Yellow River case and its argument about state-scale versus community-scale memory: Tangshan is a case where the state itself controlled, then eventually permitted, a disaster's return to public consciousness.

### Sri Lanka, 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami

Distinct enough from Aceh and Simeulue to stand on its own. The tsunami derailed a packed passenger train, one of the deadliest single rail disasters in history, and the wreck site has since become an informal memorial and, for some, a pilgrimage stop. That gives Sri Lanka a specific, concrete anchor object, a wrecked train rather than a general account of coastal devastation, that the other Indian Ocean tsunami cases in the tool don't share.

Would sit well as a fourth Indian Ocean tsunami case with its own texture, distinguishing itself from Aceh and Simeulue through this single, visitable, informally sacred site rather than through a broader community-wide narrative.

### Krakatoa and Mount Pinatubo, Philippines, 1991 eruption

Krakatoa is already in the tool as a thin case, and Mount Pinatubo could serve as its full-case companion. The 1991 eruption is notable for two distinct reasons: the displacement of the Aeta Indigenous community, who have their own oral tradition regarding the mountain, and the eruption's outsized effect on global climate that year, cooling temperatures worldwide in a way that gave it scientific as well as cultural significance. Pairing it against the already-established Krakatoa case would let the tool draw a direct line between two Indonesian-region eruptions with global atmospheric effects, one long documented and one still within living memory.

## South Asia

### Bhuj/Kutch, Gujarat, India, 2001 earthquake

An Operationalizing story with a happy ending. Post-quake recovery funding revived Kutch's traditional embroidery and handicraft economy, which is now a major livelihood and export industry in its own right, arguably stronger than before the quake. That reversal, disaster funding accidentally strengthening a cultural practice rather than merely restoring it, is uncommon enough in this research to be worth highlighting on its own.

It's a good counterweight to some of the tool's more cautionary Operationalizing cases, offering a rare example where the economic and cultural recovery from a disaster measurably outpaced the pre-disaster baseline rather than merely returning to it.

### Bhola cyclone, Bangladesh, 1970

Still the deadliest tropical cyclone on record. It inspired the 1971 Concert for Bangladesh, organized by George Harrison and Ravi Shankar, one of the first major charity benefit concerts and a direct precedent for the entire genre of celebrity disaster fundraising that later included Live Aid and similar efforts.

The cyclone also led directly to Bangladesh's now-extensive cyclone shelter and early-warning network, the same infrastructure whose volunteer arm, the Cyclone Preparedness Programme, is documented elsewhere in this research. That gives Bhola a rare direct line from a benefit song to actual infrastructure: a Formulation moment, the concert and the global attention it generated, feeding directly into decades of Operationalizing in the form of physical shelters and a trained volunteer network that still operates today.

### Nepal, 2015 Gorkha earthquake

Kathmandu Valley's Newar communities have their own centuries-old tradition of temple reconstruction, passed down through apprentice woodcarvers who rebuild damaged pagodas using inherited techniques rather than importing outside restoration methods. That distinction matters: after a major earthquake, the default global response is usually international engineering and restoration expertise, but the Newar tradition means the community already possessed its own specialists before the earthquake ever happened.

That makes Nepal a strong Strengthening and Operationalizing case about a community that already had the infrastructure to recover culturally, not just structurally: the same apprenticeship system that maintained the temples in ordinary times becomes the recovery mechanism after a disaster, rather than a new capacity that has to be built or imported after the fact.

## Middle East & Central Asia

### Ashgabat, Turkmenistan, 1948 earthquake

One of the most thoroughly suppressed disasters of the Soviet era. The death toll was classified for 40 years, foreign press was barred from the region, and seismologists were ordered not to publish real damage data. Estimates now run as high as 110,000 to 176,000 dead, a staggering range that itself reflects how little verified information ever escaped Soviet control.

About as clean a Degradation-via-political-suppression case as exists, a state-actor parallel to the tool's colonization-driven degradation examples: here it isn't foreign colonial power erasing a disaster from memory, but a state suppressing knowledge of a disaster within its own territory, for decades, from its own population as well as the outside world.

### Turkey/Syria, 2023 earthquake

Very recent, so the cultural response is still forming: murals, poetry, and an ongoing construction-fraud reckoning are all in progress but not yet settled into a documented cultural record. Worth flagging now as one to watch, and revisiting in a year or two once more material exists, since the earthquake's scale and the political controversy around building-code enforcement suggest it will eventually generate the kind of cultural record this project draws on, just not yet.

## Europe

### Messina, Italy, 1908 earthquake

At the time the deadliest earthquake in European history, with around 75,000 to 80,000 dead, but far less internationally remembered today than Lisbon 1755 or the Pompeii eruption. That gap between historical severity and present-day recognition is the case itself: a disaster that killed tens of thousands and was major global news in 1908 has simply been outshined in the long run by older or more culturally mythologized European disasters.

A good Degradation-via-being-outshined case, especially paired against Lisbon, since it demonstrates that scale of death alone doesn't guarantee lasting cultural memory; a disaster can be enormous and still lose out to other disasters in the long-term contest for collective attention.

### Skopje, North Macedonia, 1963 earthquake

Rebuilt with UN and Non-Aligned Movement solidarity aid from dozens of countries, including gifted buildings from Japan, the US, and the USSR alike, a rare Cold War-era joint effort that crossed ideological lines most other Cold War-era cooperation never did. The reconstruction itself became a point of civic identity: a city rebuilt with visible, mismatched contributions from nations that were otherwise geopolitical rivals became a symbol of solidarity that the earthquake itself, paradoxically, made possible.

That identity was later complicated by the controversial 2014 "Skopje 2014" neoclassical makeover project, which layered a nationalist architectural aesthetic over much of the mid-century reconstruction. This gives Skopje an Operationalizing case with a strange coda: a city whose rebuilt identity was defined by international solidarity later had that same built environment partially overwritten by a very different, more nationalist architectural vision, a case study in how disaster-driven memory can be architecturally erased by later political choices rather than by time alone.

### Aberfan, Wales, 1966

A coal-waste tip collapsed onto a village primary school, killing 116 children and 28 adults, in a disaster whose scale is defined less by total numbers than by exactly who died: an overwhelming share of an entire generation of one village's children, lost within minutes on an ordinary school morning.

It remains extremely present in Welsh cultural memory and still an open wound, the kind of disaster where enough time has passed for grief but not for distance. It was recently reintroduced to wider audiences through a *The Crown* episode, a case of a national streaming series bringing a still-raw local tragedy back into international visibility decades after it happened.

This is a Strengthening case, tightly localized and still raw: unlike many of the disasters in this document, Aberfan has never really needed cultural revival, since the community itself has kept it present without much interruption since 1966.

### Laki eruption, Iceland, 1783-84

Caused the "Móðuharðindin," or "Mist Hardships," famine that killed roughly a fifth of Iceland's population, an eruption whose direct kill count from lava and ash was compounded many times over by the years of crop failure and livestock death that followed the eruption's release of toxic gases across the island.

It is embedded in Icelandic literary and historical memory going back centuries, alongside the country's broader saga tradition of recording hardship, meaning Laki sits inside an existing, much older Icelandic literary practice of documenting catastrophe rather than needing a separate memorial tradition invented specifically for it. That makes it a Formulation and Strengthening case, and a rare pre-industrial climate-disaster case: the eruption's effects were as much about a poisoned atmosphere and failed harvests as about the eruption itself, giving it a slower, more agricultural character than most of the volcanic disasters elsewhere in this research.

### Venice, Italy, Acqua Alta and the MOSE flood barriers

Centuries of flooding are baked into Venetian art and identity: flooded piazzas appear constantly in painting and photography, a visual motif so common that a flooded St. Mark's Square functions almost as a genre unto itself within depictions of the city. Alongside that, an active "Venice in Peril"-style preservation culture has developed around the city's ongoing vulnerability.

That culture exists in tension with a famously troubled, decades-delayed engineering fix, the MOSE flood barrier system, whose repeated construction delays and cost overruns have themselves become part of the city's disaster narrative. Would pair naturally with the Netherlands case already in the tool, since both involve a low-lying place whose relationship with water is simultaneously its greatest cultural asset and its most persistent existential threat.

### Ireland, the Great Famine (An Gorta Mór), 1845-52

Enormous diaspora cultural memory surrounds the famine: memorials now exist across Ireland, the UK, the US, Canada, and Australia, tracing the exact routes that famine-era emigration carved across the English-speaking world. The famine shows up constantly in folk song and literature, a persistent presence across more than a century and a half of Irish and Irish-diaspora cultural production.

A useful case about disaster memory sustained primarily through a scattered diaspora rather than the place itself: unlike most of the disasters in this document, where memory is anchored to the physical site where the disaster occurred, the Great Famine's memory is arguably strongest today in cities the famine's survivors emigrated to, rather than in Ireland alone.

## Africa

### Sahel drought/famine cycles, West Africa (Mali, Niger, Chad)

Griot oral tradition carries drought memory forward generationally in a region without the same density of written record-keeping the tool currently draws on, a different mode of cultural transmission than anything currently in the tool. This case is covered in greater depth in the dedicated Africa research document, where the specific 1740s-50s "Great Famine" and the ethnomusicological research on famine-era songs are laid out at length; it's flagged here mainly to note that Bhopal, despite being geographically South Asian, is really an industrial-disaster case worth flagging on its own rather than folded into this regional set.

### Goma, Democratic Republic of Congo, Mount Nyiragongo eruptions (1977, 2002, 2021)

A city that has rebuilt directly on top of its own lava flows multiple times, across three separate eruptions spanning nearly half a century, with a local culture of ongoing, low-grade volcanic risk normalization under very difficult humanitarian conditions. This is a Formulation case, but one under real duress: unlike most of the volcanic-risk cases elsewhere in this document, Goma's population has had to normalize the volcano's danger while also managing conflict, displacement, and severe poverty, meaning the volcanic risk is only one of several overlapping crises the city's culture has had to absorb repeatedly.

## Latin America & Caribbean

### Armero, Colombia, 1985 Nevado del Ruiz eruption

A lahar, or volcanic mudflow, killed an estimated 20,000 or more people in a single night, one of the deadliest volcanic disasters of the 20th century and one made worse by warnings that reportedly went unheeded in the hours before the mudflow hit.

The photograph of Omayra Sánchez, a young girl trapped in the wreckage for three days before dying on camera, became one of the most reproduced disaster images in the world and remains a fixture of Colombian cultural memory, along with several songs written about the tragedy. Her image did for Armero something similar to what Sophia Tedro's story later did for Mozambique's floods: a single, specific human face standing in for an almost incomprehensible death toll.

A strong Formulation and Strengthening case, and a natural conversation partner for the tool's existing Haiti and Mexico City entries, since all three involve a single iconic image or story becoming the primary vehicle through which a much larger, harder-to-grasp death toll gets remembered.

### Valdivia, Chile, 1960 earthquake

The largest earthquake ever recorded, at magnitude 9.5, and deeply embedded in Chilean national memory as a defining event of the country's 20th century. Worth researching for specific cultural or literary responses before committing to a full case; the scale and national significance are well established, but the specific artistic or commemorative record that would anchor a case in this tool hasn't yet been confirmed.

### Managua, Nicaragua, 1972 earthquake

Government corruption in the aid response is widely credited as one of the sparks for the Sandinista revolution a few years later, an unusually direct line from disaster mismanagement to political upheaval. Rather than fading into ordinary disaster memory, Managua's earthquake became politically radioactive: the perceived theft and misuse of international relief aid by the Somoza government is frequently cited by historians as a factor that accelerated popular support for the revolution that eventually deposed it.

A sharp Degradation-via-institutional-failure case, distinguished from other suppression or neglect cases in this document by how directly it fed into a specific, dateable political outcome rather than simply eroding trust in the abstract.

### Yungay, Peru, 1970 Ancash earthquake and avalanche

An entire town of roughly 20,000 people was buried by a glacial avalanche in seconds, triggered by the earthquake dislodging ice and rock from a nearby peak and sending it down onto the town with almost no warning. The scale of the loss, essentially an entire town's population, all at once, distinguishes Yungay from disasters where death tolls accumulate over hours or days.

The site was left undisturbed as a memorial, known as "Campo Santo," rather than rebuilt over, one of the more literal examples anywhere of a landscape itself functioning as commemoration: no plaque, statue, or building was required, because the ground itself, left exactly as the disaster left it, does the remembering.

## North America (not yet covered)

### Dust Bowl, US Great Plains, 1930s

A surprising gap given how much cultural material exists. Woody Guthrie's *Dust Bowl Ballads*, John Steinbeck's *The Grapes of Wrath*, and Dorothea Lange's photography together form one of the most extensive bodies of disaster-response culture produced in the 20th century, spanning music, literature, and photojournalism within a few years of each other.

Probably the single most culturally documented disaster in American history that isn't yet in the tool, which makes its absence somewhat conspicuous given how much of this research project's other American material draws on comparably well-documented cases.

### Galveston, Texas, 1900 hurricane

Still the deadliest natural disaster in US history by death toll, with estimates ranging from 6,000 to 12,000 dead, a toll that remains unmatched by any hurricane, earthquake, or flood in the country's recorded history since.

It generated its own storm culture in the disaster's aftermath, and the seawall built afterward is a rare case of a US city actually restructuring itself around disaster memory in concrete and stone: rather than simply rebuilding in place, Galveston physically raised and reshaped its own geography in direct, permanent response to what the hurricane had done to it.

### Johnstown, Pennsylvania, 1889 flood

A private dam failure destroyed the town and killed over 2,200 people, a disaster caused not by a natural hazard alone but by the failure of a privately maintained dam owned by a wealthy hunting and fishing club upstream, an early American case of disaster blame falling on private negligence rather than nature alone.

The "Johnstown Flood" entered folk song almost immediately, and the disaster is often cited as an early case of press coverage shaping national disaster memory: newspaper coverage of Johnstown is frequently pointed to as one of the first instances where a disaster's national reputation was shaped as much by press narrative as by the event itself. This case connects directly to the Johnstown Flood National Memorial documented in the monuments research, where the actual failed dam site itself now serves as the memorial.

### Peshtigo, Wisconsin, 1871 fire

The deadliest wildfire in US history, with 1,500 or more dead, almost entirely forgotten because it happened the same night as the Great Chicago Fire and lost the news cycle entirely to a disaster that killed far fewer people but happened in a far more prominent city.

About as clean a Degradation-via-being-overshadowed case as exists: Peshtigo's death toll dwarfs Chicago's, yet Chicago's fire is the one that entered American popular memory, making Peshtigo a stark example of how a disaster's place in collective memory can depend more on where it happened than on how severe it was.

### New Madrid earthquakes, Missouri/Tennessee/Kentucky, 1811-12

Reportedly rang church bells in Boston, over a thousand miles from the epicenter, and was folded into regional religious folklore and camp-meeting revivalism at the time, with contemporary accounts describing the earthquakes as signs interpreted through a religious lens rather than a scientific one. Worth a research pass to confirm specifics before building out, since the most striking details, the bells in Boston chief among them, would benefit from more sourcing before anchoring a full case on them.

### Charleston, South Carolina, 1886 earthquake

Left a lasting, visible architectural adaptation: "earthquake bolts," washer-and-rod reinforcements retrofitted through brick buildings, are still visible on historic homes today and are locally recognized as a physical signature of the quake, functioning almost as an accidental plaque built into the city's own architecture rather than added afterward.

A tidy, small-scale Operationalizing case: unlike grander reconstruction efforts elsewhere in this document, Charleston's response survives today as a scattering of small hardware fixtures on individual houses, each one a tiny, distributed piece of physical memory rather than a single monument.

### Halifax, Nova Scotia, Canada, 1917 explosion

Boston sent immediate relief after the explosion, and in thanks, Nova Scotia has sent Boston an official Christmas tree nearly every year since 1918, a gesture of gratitude that was revived as an annual tradition in 1971 and formalized by the Nova Scotia government in 1976.

A rare case of gratitude, rather than warning or grief, becoming the thing that gets carried forward for over a century. Nearly every other case in this document and its companion documents concerns memory built around loss, danger, or blame; Halifax's Christmas tree tradition is instead built around thanks, repeated annually for more than a hundred years, making it a useful counterexample to the tool's more common Degradation and Strengthening narratives. This connects directly to the Halifax Explosion Memorial Bell Tower documented in the monuments research, giving the same disaster two very different kinds of ongoing commemorative practice.

### Alaska, 1964 Good Friday earthquake

The second-largest earthquake ever recorded, and Alutiiq and Chugach coastal communities carry oral histories of tsunami danger that predate any instrumental seismic record, meaning these communities possessed working knowledge of tsunami risk long before any government seismologist arrived to measure it.

A possible non-Pacific-Rim-Asia parallel to the Moken and Bajau cases already in the tool: like those communities, Alaska's Indigenous coastal populations maintained their own long-standing tsunami knowledge independent of, and predating, modern instrumental science, offering the tool a North American example of the same phenomenon it has so far only documented in Southeast Asia.

## A few worth a research pass before deciding

These came up as promising but need more digging before they're worth building out.

- **Chernobyl / Pripyat, Ukraine (1986)**: enormous cultural footprint (Svetlana Alexievich's *Chernobyl Prayer*, the HBO series, disaster tourism), but the framework fit is less clean since so much of the "culture" here is external and global rather than the affected community's own.
- **Bhopal, India (1984)**: rich activist and literary output, but closer to an industrial and corporate-negligence disaster than the tool's usual natural-hazard focus. Could still work as a deliberate outlier.
- **Christchurch, New Zealand (2011)**: strong art-based memorialization, notably the "185 empty chairs" installation, worth a closer look at whether there's more underneath it.
- **Assam, India**: Brahmaputra flood cycles tied to festival timing, promising but thin in what's been checked so far.
