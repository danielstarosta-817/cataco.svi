## The tradition itself, in Griggs's own sourcing

New Orleans journalist Chris Rose, describing Hurricane Georges in 1998, told NPR that he and his neighbors emptied their refrigerators of perishables for a two-day neighborhood cookout, stopping intermittently to hit golf balls into the gale-force winds at the nearby course. "A hurricane party," Rose said, "[is] a community affair."

Before Hurricane Isidore in 2002, the New Orleans Journal polled residents on how they were preparing, and one woman read off her grocery list as "apple-turnovers, pound cake, pecan bread, banana bread, beer, tuna salad, ham salad… and beer. Lots of beer." That's not just one household's idiosyncrasy: grocery and liquor store alcohol sales reliably spike each season when a storm is announced.

Rose, again, spoke to the scale of the practice: "Nobody keeps count of how many residents participate in such events, but safe to say, it's a lot of us, maybe even most of us who are left after you subtract the evacuees." The practice isn't confined to the Gulf either. Griggs cites hurricane parties reported as far north as New England, and a 2011 New York Post piece describing New Yorkers stocking their liquor cabinets ahead of Hurricane Irene "in hopes of getting blotto."

## Real origins, with real texture

Griggs traces the tradition to just before World War II, when improved hurricane-resistant construction and better meteorological forecasting let residents in a storm's path stay and ride it out rather than face a purely evacuate-or-die choice, especially in an era with no modern interstate system to evacuate on anyway.

Prohibition's 1933 repeal is the other half of it. The 1930s and '40s saw a broader normalization of drinking culture generally, the at-home cocktail party, the neighborhood bar, the three-martini lunch, and safely-housed Gulf Coast residents folded legal alcohol directly into storm prep. By the mid-1930s, hurricanes were colloquially graded by drink count, "one-quart," "two-quart," or "three-quart" storms, depending on how much liquor a household expected to get through riding it out. The Columbia Record reported in 1935 that locals would "lock themselves in a safe place and get 'blind' until it is over."

Newspaper accounts of wild hurricane parties proliferate starting around 1950. A Miamian told the Baltimore Sun in 1952, "During a hurricane, have a party!" The Times-Picayune declared "there's no better show than a rootin', tootin' hurricane and no more fun-filled experience than a hurricane party." These weren't only informal home gatherings either: in 1952, an upscale Miami hotel caught by an unexpected storm gave guests free lodging and threw its own hurricane party, food, drinks, and entertainment included, at no charge.

## Not just a rich person's indulgence, and not always trivial

Griggs is specific that hurricane parties ran across class lines: cafes, bars, military bases, oil rigs, and dorm rooms all hosted versions of the same ritual, not just private homes.

She cites a striking 1981 New York Amsterdam News retrospective by Lionel Mitchell, describing a 1962 hurricane party as one of the only interracial gathering spaces available under Jim Crow-era policing: "It was the first time students, liberals, faculty couples, bohemians, musicians, creative people could get together under cover of the 100 mile-an-hour winds on an interracial basis with no fear of police raids or reprisals of any sort."

A storm, in that account, briefly suspended the usual enforcement of segregation, turning a hazard into cover for a kind of gathering that the surrounding social order otherwise wouldn't allow.

## The sharpest thread in the piece, and the one worth taking most seriously

Griggs draws a hard line between who gets to treat a hurricane as a party and who doesn't. The Philadelphia Tribune reported in 1969, after Hurricane Camille destroyed the homes of many poor Black residents on the Mississippi Coast: "Negroes in Mississippi do not have 'hurricane parties' when such storms come through their communities. Such 'parties' are for wealthy white folk."

Black residents of the region, the Tribune wrote, instead held "praying parties." Many had retreated inland to survive Camille and returned to find their property in ruins, a materially different experience of the same storm than the one being celebrated at parties elsewhere.

Griggs's own conclusion is blunt and worth preserving directly: "When it comes to hurricanes, the ability to be vulnerable and emerge unscathed is an immense privilege." This reframes the whole tradition for the tool: the same performed bravado that reads as communal joy in one account is, in a different community hit by the same storm, simply not an available posture, because there's no guarantee of a home to come back to.

## The cautionary legend the tradition eventually produced about itself

The Richelieu Manor Apartments story became hurricane-party folklore's most famous cautionary tale. Residents of the newly built complex in Pass Christian, Mississippi, allegedly held a hurricane party in 1969 despite repeated evacuation warnings as Camille approached, and the sole reported survivor, Mary Ann Gerlach, said she survived by clinging to a sofa cushion after the storm surge pushed her out a second-story window.

Griggs notes the story's veracity has since been seriously questioned. That didn't matter for the tradition's own regulation, however: media outlets and municipal governments took the tale and turned it into the standard cautionary example cited to convince residents to heed official evacuation orders.

It's a folk legend doing real disaster-risk-communication work regardless of how much of it actually happened, the hurricane-party tradition generating its own internal warning story about the limits of exactly the bravado it otherwise celebrates.

## The climate framing, as Griggs actually made it

Written days after Tropical Storm Imelda dumped 43 inches of rain on southeast Texas, the piece cites a "detectable" century-long increase in Atlantic hurricane activity and links climate change directly to more extreme rainfall events and to Hurricane Harvey's destructive power in Houston (2017, 75-plus deaths).

A teenage climate protester at Houston's City Hall is quoted directly: "Our so-called natural disasters are no longer entirely natural… this is what being on the front lines of climate change looks like."

Despite that, Griggs argues the parties persist precisely because of the community they foster against fear, regardless of the growing risk. Pre-Harvey social media posts boasted of "early-morning beer" runs; one Twitter user wrote, "I got beer, wine, rum, peanut butter & crackers. We may be floating on a mattress with squirrels, but at least it'll be fun!" Hurricane parties were reported through Isaac (2012), Matthew (2016), and Harvey, Irma, and Nate (2017) alike.

Griggs's own closing framing acknowledges the limits of the tradition directly: hurricane parties, in her words, are "a momentary means to cope with the hopelessness this crisis can bring. They're a blip of joy, perhaps, amid anxiety and chaos." The piece ends on the same 2002 New Orleans Journal survey it opened with, one respondent's memory of a grandfather who "loved hurricanes," threw a plywood-shuttered party every time one came, and told his guests, "Enjoy the wind, enjoy the wind."

## The rapid-intensification thread, still worth keeping as a separate addition

Griggs's own piece is about a general, century-long increase in storm activity and rainfall extremity, not specifically about storms outrunning their own warning time. That's an important distinction for the tool to keep straight, since the mechanism below comes from a different, separately sourced line of research rather than from Griggs's article itself.

That more specific mechanism is storms strengthening much faster in the final day or two before landfall: roughly twice as many North Atlantic storms have undergone rapid intensification in the last two decades as fifty years ago, intensifying about 28% faster on average. This still stands as a real addition from the first research pass, distinct from anything in the Eater piece.

The hurricane party tradition assumes enough lead time to plan a stay-and-ride-it-out gathering around a storm of known strength, and a storm that jumps two categories overnight breaks that assumption without anyone at the party necessarily noticing the tradition itself has become riskier than the version their grandparents practiced.
