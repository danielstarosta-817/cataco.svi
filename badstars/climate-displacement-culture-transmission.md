# Transmitting a living culture through climate displacement

Four cases, tied together by a different framing than almost everything else in this research: not a culture remembering a disaster that already happened, but a culture actively trying to carry itself forward through a slow-motion, still-unfolding one, sea-level rise and land loss that will outlast any single storm. Two are about literal, forced relocation (Isle de Jean Charles, and the Ambae evacuations behind Vanuatu's program); two are about revival, teaching a nearly-lost land-management system back into practice because it may be the actual answer to the crisis rather than just a way of grieving it (KASL, the ahupua'a work).

## Outline

- **Kwajalein Atoll Sustainability Laboratory (KASL), Marshall Islands:** a Marshallese-run NGO founded in 2022 on an island less than three meters above sea level, building climate-adaptation infrastructure and offline education resources aimed at a generation that may never live on the atoll as adults.
- **Wokbaot Wetem Kalja ("moving with culture"), Vanuatu:** a 2021 to 2024 applied research project that used the repeated evacuation of Ambae island to argue relocation should be led by customary chiefs rather than outside authorities, a finding written into national policy in 2018.
- **Isle de Jean Charles, Louisiana:** often called the first federally funded climate relocation in US history, completed in 2022 and still fractured by an unresolved dispute over who counts as a member of the resettled community.
- **The ahupua'a system, Hawaii:** a precolonial, mountain-to-sea land-management system now being actively revived by a Native Hawaiian-led nonprofit network as a genuine climate-resilience strategy, not merely a heritage exercise.

## Kwajalein Atoll Sustainability Laboratory (KASL), Marshall Islands, founded 2022

Directly deepens the tool's existing, currently-dimmed Marshall Islands case (stick-chart wave-finding knowledge), and updates it from a purely historical entry into an active, present-tense one. KASL is a Marshallese-run NGO based on Ebeye, a Kwajalein Atoll island that sits less than three meters above sea level, built inside the new College of the Marshall Islands campus specifically to host climate-adaptation field research with direct local involvement rather than outside experts parachuting in.

Its first major project, "Jebake Nest," is explicitly three things at once: an environmental sensor station collecting real local climate data, an offline community education resource teaching science and climate literacy to Marshallese residents, and a physical shelter. The offline design is a deliberate choice, meaning the resource still functions if the atoll's connectivity fails during an actual disaster, when a purely web-based tool would go dark exactly when it's needed most.

The stated cultural stakes are as direct as anything in this research. Marshallese leaders describe a climate-driven diaspora already underway, and frame remaining Marshallese, wherever people end up living, as the actual goal, meaning KASL's youth education mission is explicitly aimed at people who may never get to live on these islands as adults.

## Wokbaot Wetem Kalja ("moving with culture"), Vanuatu, applied research project, 2021 to 2024

The clearest single match to the framing of helping transmit a living culture while the country it comes from is disappearing. Run by the International Organization for Migration with Vanuatu's Malvatumauri National Council of Chiefs and the Vanuatu Cultural Centre, the project used the 2017 to 2019 double evacuation of Ambae island, evacuated, allowed to return, then evacuated again as the Manaro volcano kept erupting, as its real-world case study for a broader question: how do you relocate people without breaking the customary social structure that makes a community actually function.

Since roughly 97% of Vanuatu's land operates under kastom, customary, chief-negotiated tenure rather than state title, any relocation is inherently a cultural negotiation, not just a logistics problem. The project's core finding was to deliberately empower customary chiefs and institutions to lead those negotiations themselves rather than have government or aid agencies impose relocation terms from outside.

This directly informed Vanuatu's 2018 National Climate Change and Disaster-Induced Displacement Policy, which formally commits the state to respecting custom and protecting traditional environmental knowledge through displacement. It is a rare case of an oral or customary governance system getting written directly into national climate policy rather than being treated as an obstacle to it.

## Isle de Jean Charles, Louisiana, resettlement completed 2022, ongoing dispute since

Often cited as the first federally funded climate relocation in US history, and worth including specifically for the honest, unresolved tension in it rather than as a clean success story. The Biloxi-Chitimacha-Choctaw community's island home in Terrebonne Parish has lost the large majority of its land to subsidence, leveed-off sediment, and saltwater intrusion, and the state used a federal grant to buy 515 acres near Schriever for a resettlement community residents named "the New Isle."

The access dispute is the part most relevant to the question of cultural transmission. Tribal leadership says the original understanding was that any tribal member who'd left the island as early as the 1960s, not just current residents, would have a right to move to the new community, since the tribe's identity was never supposed to be defined by whoever happened to still be living on a shrinking sandbar.

Louisiana's state housing office disagreed, restricting eligibility mainly to people still on the island or displaced by a 2012 hurricane, a decision that fractured the relationship between the tribe and the state and left some tribal members with no formal claim to the community meant to carry their own culture forward. Worth treating as a genuine cautionary case alongside KASL and Vanuatu: even a well-funded, good-faith relocation can still get the cultural-continuity question wrong if the state, rather than the community itself, ends up deciding who counts as "in."

## The ahupua'a system, Hawaii, precolonial in origin, active revival ongoing

Distinct from the other three in that this isn't primarily about people who've already been displaced. It is about reviving a land-management system specifically because it may be a genuine climate-resilience answer, not just a heritage exercise. An ahupua'a is a wedge-shaped land division running from mountain to sea, uplands managed for water capture and forestry, midlands for taro and farming, coastline for fishponds and reef fisheries, designed so a single community held every climate and resource zone it needed within one self-sufficient unit.

Kuaʻāina Ulu ʻAuamo (KUA), a Native Hawaiian-led nonprofit formalized in 2013 at the direct request of fishers and farmers from more than 30 rural communities, now coordinates three statewide networks practicing pieces of this system today: E Alu Pū (40+ community groups), Hui Mālama Loko Iʻa (60+ traditional fishpond restoration projects), and the Limu Hui (native seaweed practitioners), organized around the concept of ʻāina momona, a productive, abundant ecological system that supports the people living on it.

Ahupuaʻa O Kahana State Park on Oʻahu functions as a rare, still-intact, publicly accessible example of the whole system at once, roughly 5,300 acres from fishpond to forest, kept specifically so the practices can still be taught rather than only described. Worth flagging the direct tie-in already sitting in the tool: this is the same land-and-water ethic underneath the live Lahaina/Maui case's pre-plantation water system, meaning the ahupua'a material could either become its own case or fold directly into deepening Maui's existing one.

## Why these four belong together

Read as a set, they cover nearly the full range of what transmitting a living culture through displacement can actually mean in practice: building the next generation's own scientific and educational capacity in place before the water wins (KASL), formally empowering customary authority to manage the social side of relocation itself (Vanuatu), the real, unresolved difficulty of deciding who a relocated community even is once it's been physically broken apart (Isle de Jean Charles), and reviving an entire precolonial resource-management system because it might out-perform whatever replaced it (the ahupuaʻa). None of these are about a disaster that already happened and needs remembering. All four are about a culture trying to stay itself while the ground, sometimes literally, keeps moving.
