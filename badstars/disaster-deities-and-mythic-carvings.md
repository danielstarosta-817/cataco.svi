# Gods, flood myths, and the carvings that came out of them

## Outline

- The Aztec Sun Stone (Piedra del Sol): a 24-ton basalt disc encoding the Five Suns cosmology, in which four earlier worlds were each destroyed by a different catastrophe before the present age.
- Tlaloc: the Aztec rain and storm god's 168-ton monolith, the largest monolithic sculpture in the Americas, whose 1964 move to Mexico City was greeted by an unseasonal storm.
- Chaac: more than 200 stone mosaic masks of the Maya rain god cover the Governor's Palace at Uxmal, in a region with no surface rivers and total dependence on rainfall.
- Chalchiuhtlicue: the Aztec goddess of water and floods, credited with ending the Fourth Sun, who also governed childbirth and purification.
- Hapi and Sekhmet: Egyptian deities of the Nile flood and of plague, the latter appeased by Amenhotep III with several hundred statues.
- Poseidon: the Greek sea god's epithet "earth-shaker" tied him to earthquakes as much as to the sea, especially in seismically active regions like Corinth.
- Deucalion and Pyrrha: the Greek flood myth, structurally near-identical to the Mesopotamian and biblical flood stories, revived across centuries of art.
- The Descent of the Ganges, Mahabalipuram: one of the largest rock reliefs on earth, depicting Shiva easing the Ganges to earth to prevent catastrophic flooding.
- Matsya: Vishnu's fish avatar, the Hindu parallel to Noah, warning Manu of a coming deluge.
- Fuxi and Nüwa: the Chinese myth of Gong Gong's flood and Nüwa's repair of the sky, carved at the 2nd-century Wu Liang Shrine alongside a separate relief of the flood-taming king Yu.
- Susanoo, Raijin, and Fujin: Japanese storm and thunder deities immortalized in Tawaraya Sōtatsu's famous 17th-century folding screen.

This is the sixth research pass, sitting between the visual art and monuments documents. Rather than paintings or built plaques and festivals, the focus here is the deities that communities invented specifically to explain, personify, or beg protection from a hazard, and the sculpture and relief carving that grew out of worshipping them. The Aztec Sun Stone is the anchor example, flagged specifically for this pass, and the scope runs from antiquity through the 19th century, deep time first, matching the ordering of the other documents.

**The Aztec Sun Stone (Piedra del Sol), Mexico, early 1500s, pre-conquest.** The Sun Stone is a 24-ton basalt disc, already covered in the visual art document, but it belongs here too as the core example of this research pass: in most interpretations, its central imagery reads as the Five Suns cosmology.

That cosmology holds that four earlier worlds existed before the present one, and each was destroyed by its own specific catastrophe: the first by jaguars, the second by hurricane-force wind, the third by a rain of fire, and the fourth by a flood that turned all of humanity into fish. The present age is the fifth sun, built on top of that repeated cycle of destruction.

What makes the Sun Stone useful as an anchor for this whole research pass is what it does with that idea structurally: it treats the destruction of entire worlds by hazard as the basic architecture of time itself, four full cycles of catastrophe and rebirth before the present moment, rather than as a one-time aberration interrupting an otherwise stable order.

**Tlaloc, Aztec and earlier Mesoamerican rain and storm god.** The Monolith of Tlaloc is a 168-ton, 23-foot basalt carving, discovered in the 1880s near Coatlinchan and moved to Mexico City in 1964, where it now stands outside the National Museum of Anthropology as the largest monolithic sculpture anywhere in the Americas.

Tlaloc's domain covered rain, lightning, and flooding all at once, meaning drought and deluge were understood as the same god's responsibility rather than opposite forces. That combined power came with a harsh corollary: Tlaloc reportedly demanded child sacrifice specifically at moments when the rains failed, tying the god's goodwill directly to the community's water supply.

The monolith's move to the capital produced its own postscript worth keeping on record: it arrived during the dry season in 1964, greeted by a crowd of 25,000 people, and an unseasonal storm broke out just as it reached the city. Plenty of locals read the timing as exactly what it appeared to be, a sign that the rain god's power had traveled with his statue.

**Chaac, Maya rain god, Yucatán.** The Governor's Palace at Uxmal carries more than 200 stone mosaic masks of Chaac across its facade, assembled out of more than 15,000 individually carved stone pieces.

Art historians have described that sheer repetition as "a monumental prayer in stone for rain," and the stakes behind the phrase are literal rather than poetic: the Yucatán peninsula has no surface rivers, so the entire region depends on seasonal rainfall and the cenotes it fills. A failed rainy season there was an existential hazard, not a seasonal inconvenience.

Read against that dependence, the building itself functions as a direct, physical, repeated appeal against the one risk that mattered most to the people who built it: hundreds of individually carved faces, each one another plea against the same failure of rain.

**Chalchiuhtlicue, Aztec goddess of water, lakes, and floods.** Chalchiuhtlicue is the same deity credited with ending the Fourth Sun in the flood myth carved into the Sun Stone described above.

Her domain extended beyond destruction: she also ruled over childbirth and baptism-like purification rites, meaning the same goddess governed water as both life-giving and world-ending, the two functions held by a single figure rather than split across separate deities.

**Hapi, Egyptian god of the Nile flood, and Sekhmet, goddess of plague and destruction.** Hapi already appears in the monuments and events documents as the personification behind the nilometers and the Wafaa El-Nil festival, but the sculptural record deserves its own mention here: temple reliefs at Karnak and elsewhere depict Hapi as an androgynous, fertile figure physically carrying the floodwaters.

Sekhmet is the harder case in this group: a lioness-headed goddess of plague and violent destruction. According to the "Destruction of Mankind" myth, she was sent by the sun god Ra to punish humanity and came close to wiping it out entirely before being tricked into stopping.

Amenhotep III responded to that mythology by commissioning several hundred statues of Sekhmet, by some estimates one for each day of the year, for his mortuary temple. That is an entire sculptural program built around appeasing a goddess of precisely the kind of destruction a community hopes never actually arrives, an insurance policy carved in stone at massive scale.

**Poseidon, Greek god of the sea and, specifically, earthquakes.** Poseidon carried the epithet Ennosigaios, "earth-shaker," reflecting a second domain distinct from his maritime one: he was also the god held responsible for earthquakes.

Cities in the seismically active parts of Greece built temples to him specifically for that earthquake-related role rather than his connection to the sea. The Corinth region, a notably earthquake-prone part of the ancient Greek world, held Poseidon in particularly high civic regard for exactly that reason.

**Deucalion and Pyrrha, the Greek flood myth.** In this myth, Zeus floods the earth to destroy a humanity he considers corrupt. Deucalion and Pyrrha survive by riding out the flood inside a wooden chest, and afterward repopulate the world in a strange, specific way: by throwing stones over their shoulders, which turn into people.

The story is structurally almost identical to Utnapishtim's flood in the Epic of Gilgamesh and to the Genesis flood, three separate ancient traditions converging on the same basic shape of survival narrative.

The myth was depicted across Greek and later Roman vase painting and relief, and it was revived constantly once classical mythology came back into fashion during the Renaissance and Baroque periods, giving it a visual afterlife stretching from antiquity through early modern European painting.

**The Descent of the Ganges, Mahabalipuram, India, 7th century CE.** The Descent of the Ganges is one of the largest rock reliefs on earth, spanning two granite boulders roughly 50 by 100 feet.

It depicts the myth of Shiva catching the full force of the descending Ganges in his hair, breaking the river into divided streams so it could reach the earth gently rather than as a single destructive torrent that would have flattened everything below it, a myth that is essentially a story about flood mitigation encoded into religious narrative.

The site reportedly once had a water tank cut into the rock above the relief, so that during festivals real water could be released to cascade down over the carving itself. That would have turned the monument into a working reenactment of its own myth, water physically flowing over a carving of a god managing the flow of water.

**Matsya, the fish avatar of Vishnu, and the flood of Manu.** Matsya is the Hindu parallel to Utnapishtim and Noah: Vishnu takes the form of a fish to warn the first man, Manu, of a coming deluge, instructing him to build a boat and gather the seeds of all living things before the flood arrives.

The story has been carved and painted across many centuries of Hindu temple sculpture and later miniature painting traditions, giving it a continuous visual record stretching across a very long span of time.

Matsya makes Hinduism one of at least three separate, geographically unconnected ancient cultures, alongside the Mesopotamian and arguably the Mesoamerican traditions, that each independently encoded the same basic instruction into their foundational religious literature: build a vessel, save what you can, and survive the flood.

**Fuxi and Nüwa, and the flood caused by Gong Gong, China, myth ancient, Wu Liang Shrine reliefs 2nd century CE.** In this myth, the water god Gong Gong smashes his head against Mount Buzhou, one of the pillars holding up the sky, tilting the heavens and unleashing a catastrophic flood across the earth.

Nüwa repairs the broken sky using five-colored stones and stops the flooding by propping the world back up with the legs of a giant turtle. The Wu Liang Shrine in Shandong, built around 151 CE, carries a stone relief of Fuxi and Nüwa together, serpent-bodied and human-headed, holding a carpenter's square and a compass, among the oldest surviving examples of figural art from China.

The same shrine complex separately carries a relief of Yu the Great, the legendary flood-taming king, meaning one small building preserves two distinct, ancient Chinese flood narratives side by side: the myth of the flood's cause and the myth of the flood's eventual taming.

**Susanoo, Raijin, and Fujin, Japanese storm and thunder deities.** Susanoo is the Shinto god of storms and the sea, and Raijin and Fujin are a paired wind-and-thunder duo, together representing a tradition separate from the namazu-e catfish prints covered elsewhere in this research: older, and more purely religious than the mostly satirical Edo-period woodblock print genre.

Tawaraya Sōtatsu's folding-screen painting "Wind God and Thunder God," made in the 17th century, is one of the most famous images in all of Japanese art and has been copied by generations of later painters, giving these two deities an enormous visual legacy.

That legacy sits closer to devotional and decorative art than to a direct disaster-response artifact, worth noting as the more ambiguous case in this set: a great and enduring work of art built around gods of storm and thunder, but not necessarily produced in direct reaction to any single documented weather disaster.
