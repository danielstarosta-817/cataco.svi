# Deliberate, top-down celebrity and mascot preparedness campaigns

Eighth research pass, a distinct category from everything else in these documents. Almost everything so far has been culture that emerged from a community after or around a disaster, organically or semi-organically. This one is the opposite direction: institutions (governments, the World Bank, NGOs) deliberately hiring celebrities, athletes, or inventing a mascot specifically to change behavior before a disaster hits. Confirmed the campaign you flagged and went looking for real parallels, being upfront about which ones actually line up and which don't.

## Outline

- **Disaster Fighters Campaign, Caribbean (2021)**: a CDEMA/World Bank musical PSA featuring cricket legends and regional musicians reached 20 million people across 19 countries and was tested with an actual randomized controlled experiment.
- **Smokey Bear, United States (since 1944)**: the historical ancestor of this whole category, a wartime-invented cartoon bear whose "Only YOU can prevent forest fires" slogan became the longest-running PSA campaign in American history.
- **Cyclone Preparedness Programme, Bangladesh (since 1973)**: a volunteer network of over 76,000 trained locals delivers cyclone warnings by megaphone, siren, and radio to coastal communities with no other way to receive them.
- **Where I came up short**: no confirmed sports-celebrity-fronted preparedness campaign outside the Caribbean was found, despite the Philippines, India, and Australia all seeming like likely candidates.

## The full entries

### Disaster Fighters Campaign, Caribbean, 2021

Confirmed and even bigger than the initial figures suggested. CDEMA, the Caribbean Disaster Emergency Management Agency, with backing from the World Bank and the Global Facility for Disaster Reduction and Recovery (GFDRR), filmed a musical PSA across Anguilla, Grenada, Haiti, Saint Lucia, and Trinidad and Tobago. The cast paired cricket legends, Sir Garfield Sobers, Darren Sammy, Kieran Powell, and Omari Banks, with regional musical artists, Mr. Killa, Ricky T, Tafa Mi Soleil, and QPID, putting some of the region's most recognizable sporting and musical names behind a single unified message.

The song's lyrics are plain, functional preparedness instructions set to music: check your flashlight, trim your trees, know your nearest shelter, check on elderly neighbors. There is no metaphor or narrative layered over the content; the entertainment value is the delivery mechanism for a checklist that would otherwise arrive as a government pamphlet nobody reads.

It reportedly reached 20 million people across 19 countries, and, notably, the World Bank actually ran a randomized controlled experiment on it, finding that the musical format measurably increased how much people trusted and liked the message and how confident they felt about acting on it. It is rare to see a cultural intervention get an actual RCT behind it rather than just anecdotal buy-in, which makes this campaign an unusually well-evidenced example of celebrity messaging changing measurable attitudes rather than just generating views.

### Smokey Bear, United States, since 1944

The obvious historical ancestor of this whole category, and worth having as the anchor comparison. The US Forest Service and the Ad Council invented a cartoon bear specifically as wildfire-prevention messaging during World War II, when real bears and real forestry staff were both in short supply and wildfire risk to war-relevant timber was a genuine strategic concern.

The character was given his defining line, "Only YOU can prevent forest fires," in 1947, later updated to "wildfires" in 2001 to reflect that fires start in more than just forests. The campaign built around that single mascot and slogan is now the longest-running PSA campaign in American history, and close to 80% of Americans can still identify Smokey by image alone, a recognition rate most modern advertising campaigns never approach.

Congress passed a dedicated law in 1952 putting his image under federal control specifically so campaign royalties could keep funding wildfire education, an institutional commitment to a single mascot that has now outlasted most of the people who created him. That legal scaffolding is part of what makes Smokey different from a normal advertising character: he is not owned the way a company owns a logo, he is legislated, which has let the campaign persist across decades of shifting media and changing wildfire science.

### Cyclone Preparedness Programme, Bangladesh, since 1973

A different mechanism entirely, no celebrities or songs confirmed, but worth naming as the volunteer-network version of the same underlying goal. Over 76,000 trained local volunteers, roughly half of them women, cover the 13 coastal districts most exposed to cyclone risk, working with megaphones, hand-cranked sirens, and radios rather than any broadcast media campaign.

What makes the program distinctive is its reach into places no media campaign could touch: remote coastal chars and islands with no mobile signal, reached by volunteers broadcasting warnings from boats or from mosque minarets. It is less a media campaign than a permanent, embedded, human early-warning infrastructure, the opposite design philosophy from a viral music video, but aimed at exactly the same outcome of getting people to act before a storm arrives rather than after.

### Where I came up short

I went looking specifically for a sports-celebrity-fronted preparedness campaign outside the Caribbean. The Philippines, India, or Australia seemed like the likeliest candidates given how disaster-prone and sports-mad all three are, and I didn't find a clean match in the time I spent searching. That's a real gap worth another look, ideally starting from a specific agency, a national disaster management office, rather than the sport or celebrity angle, since that is more likely to surface a campaign that exists but simply isn't indexed under celebrity or sports coverage.
