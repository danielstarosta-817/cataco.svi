Raised Under Bad Stars
Strengthening · making it stick

New Orleans

Hurricane Katrina displaced over a million people in 2005 and left psychological damage that outlasted the physical rebuild. The city answered with tools decades old: a flood song, a spray-painted code, and a culture that refused to wait for permission to start healing.

I.

A song written fifty years late, and thirty years early

Louisiana, Louisiana—they're trying to wash us away.Randy Newman, "Louisiana 1927"

Randy Newman didn't write "Louisiana 1927" as an immediate response to anything. He wrote it more than fifty years after the Great Mississippi Flood it describes, after learning his own mother was from New Orleans and getting curious about the region's history: "it was the other place that I knew, and I was interested in the history, and heard about this flood, and I wrote the song." For decades afterward it stayed a period piece, a song about something that happened a long time ago to people who weren't around to hear it.

Then Katrina hit, and the city adopted the song as its own post-Katrina anthem, the flood and the callous institutional response it describes suddenly no longer historical. New Orleans native Aaron Neville, who had performed the song for years, put the shift plainly: "when I used to sing that song, it was about something that happened a long time ago. Now when I sing it, it's about something that happened to me and my family, so it's a lot more real." A song written to be historical became, without changing a word, a song about the person singing it.

II.

Finding a voice

Katrina's psychological toll did not peak on landfall. It kept climbing for years. PTSD rates in affected regions rose from 15% a few months after the storm to 21% a year later. Twelve years on, roughly one in six residents still showed symptoms consistent with probable PTSD.

Disaster mental health has leaned increasingly on creative arts therapy, precisely because it can meet people where their own cultural vocabulary already is, instead of importing a clinical language that may not translate. The community cohesion people feel right after a disaster can collapse fast, and it is in that gap that locally rooted cultural practice can do work a generic protocol cannot.

III.

The X on the door

Search-and-rescue teams marked every home they checked with a spray-painted X, a system developed by FEMA's Urban Search and Rescue program that packed real information into four quadrants: the date and time of the search, which team searched it, what hazards were found inside, and how many people, living or dead, were found. It was never meant to be a piece of public art. It was a working code, left behind for whoever came through next.

Many homeowners kept the X anyway once they returned. In Bywater, artist Erica Larkin Gaudet recast her partner's X into an iron sculpture he called his house's "Katrina Stigmata," a tattoo for having made it, he said, describing a mark left by first responders as something closer to a badge than a scar. In Chalmette, Carol Shoemaker commissioned a roller-derby teammate to render her home's neon-orange X in ironwork: "not like a tattoo that I see every day, but part of the history of the house." The word residents kept reaching for, on their houses if not their own skin, was tattoo — a mark you don't take off, whether or not you'd have chosen it.

IV.

Racing to record it while it was still fresh

Within months of the storm, multiple oral history projects moved to record New Orleanians in their own words before memory had time to settle. The Hurricane Digital Memory Bank grew into the largest free public archive of Katrina and Rita material anywhere: more than 25,000 items. None of these projects waited for the story to become history first. They treated the act of recording itself as part of the recovery.

Where the culture went next

HBO's Treme picked up the story after most of the cameras had already left: musicians, chefs, Mardi Gras Indians, and ordinary residents rebuilding their lives, and their culture, in Katrina's slow, unglamorous aftermath. It treated the city's culture itself as the thing worth watching rebuild, not just the levees.
