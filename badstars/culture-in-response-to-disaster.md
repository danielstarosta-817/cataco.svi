# Concrete art, music, and literature made in response to disasters

## Outline

**Strengthens cases already in the tool**
- Great Mississippi Flood, 1927: Faulkner's 1939 novella "Old Man" follows a convict ordered to rescue a pregnant woman during the flood and is still taught alongside his Nobel Prize-winning body of work.
- South Florida hurricanes: Hemingway's 1935 essay "Who Murdered the Vets?" accused the government of responsibility for the deaths of veterans stranded in Florida Keys work camps during the Labor Day Hurricane.

- Cumberland River flood, Nashville, 1926: Bessie Smith's "Back-Water Blues" is almost universally misattributed to the Mississippi flood of 1927 rather than the smaller, forgotten flood it actually describes.
- Galveston hurricane, 1900: "Wasn't That a Mighty Storm" traveled from an oral church spiritual to a Library of Congress recording to the folk revival to a 2009 James Taylor cover.

A follow-up to `candidate-places.md`, this time hunting for the Hurston/Okeechobee kind of thing specifically: a named, datable work, tied to a named, datable disaster, with actual evidence it entered and stayed in cultural memory (revivals, covers, prizes, canonization, museum collections, continued teaching), rather than simply being part of local lore. Organized by disaster, split into two groups: things that could deepen cases already in the tool, and things that make the case for building a new one.

## Strengthens cases already in the tool

**Great Mississippi Flood, 1927 (the tool's `levee-breaks` case).** William Faulkner's 1939 novella "Old Man," published inside the two-story volume *The Wild Palms*, follows a convict ordered to rescue a pregnant woman as the floodwaters rise around them, structuring the disaster around a single forced act of rescue carried out by a man with no real choice in the matter.

The novella has stayed inside the working literary canon rather than fading into a period-piece footnote. It is still routinely taught alongside the rest of Faulkner's body of work, the same body of work that won him the Nobel Prize in Literature.

Worth stacking directly next to the tool's existing "When the Levee Breaks" material as a second response to the identical 1927 flood, this time literary rather than musical: a convict's forced rescue narrative sitting alongside a blues record, two very different registers describing the same several weeks of flooding along the Mississippi.

**South Florida hurricanes (the tool's `hurricane-culture` case).** On September 17, 1935, just two weeks after the Labor Day Hurricane tore through the Florida Keys, Ernest Hemingway published "Who Murdered the Vets?" in *New Masses*. The hurricane killed several hundred World War I veterans who had been sent to work camps in the Keys and left there without evacuation as the storm approached.

The piece functions less as a disaster narrative than as an accusation: Hemingway asks directly who was responsible for stranding those men, aiming the question at the government agencies that had put them there and failed to get them out in time.

It has stayed in circulation long enough that a socialist-press retrospective marked its 90th anniversary this year, nearly a century after the storm itself. It stands as a sharp example of literature functioning as accountability rather than commemoration, closer to journalism aimed at assigning blame than to elegy.

**Lisbon, 1755 (the tool's `lisbon` case).** The Lisbon earthquake produced two of Voltaire's most enduring works: his poem "Poème sur le désastre de Lisbonne" in 1756, and three years later the novel *Candide*, both wrestling directly with the disaster and using it to attack the philosophical idea that this is "the best of all possible worlds."

*Candide* in particular has held its place in the canon rather than aging into obscurity. It remains one of the most widely taught works in the Western literary tradition, still assigned in classrooms nearly 270 years after the earthquake that provoked it.

Less famous but just as concrete is Immanuel Kant's response: in 1756 he wrote three scientific essays attempting to explain the earthquake's causes through natural rather than divine mechanisms. Those essays are sometimes credited as a founding moment of modern seismology, meaning the Lisbon earthquake produced both a landmark of world literature and an early attempt at treating earthquakes as a subject for science.

**Mexico City, 1985 (the tool's `mexico-city` case).** Elena Poniatowska's *Nada, nadie: las voces del temblor*, published in 1988, assembles an oral history of the earthquake out of survivor testimony, much of it collected through a writing workshop Poniatowska ran herself with local women in the disaster's aftermath.

The book pairs that first-person testimony with a documented account of the government's botched response, giving voice to residents whose experience of the earthquake was shaped as much by official failure as by the shaking itself.

It is now considered a classic of Mexican nonfiction, and Poniatowska went on to win the Miguel de Cervantes Prize, the highest honor given in Spanish-language literature. It sits naturally alongside the mural and displacement-camp material already documented elsewhere in that case, giving the 1985 earthquake a written testimony to go with its visual and physical traces.

## Makes the case for a new one

**Cumberland River flood, Nashville, Tennessee, 1926.** Bessie Smith recorded "Back-Water Blues" in February 1927 and released it that March, timing that led to an almost universal, and almost entirely wrong, assumption: because the record came out just as the Great Mississippi Flood was dominating the news, generations of listeners have taken the song to be about that flood.

Musicologist David Evans has traced it instead to a different, much less remembered flood: Nashville's Christmas Day flood of 1926, which sent the Cumberland River over its banks and displaced roughly 10,000 people, most of them Black residents.

The mismatch makes "Back-Water Blues" close to a perfect case study for the degradation and decontextualization theme running through this research: a real song about a real, now largely forgotten flood, permanently misfiled in public memory under a bigger, more famous one that simply happened to be in the news at the same moment.

**Galveston hurricane, Texas, 1900.** "Wasn't That a Mighty Storm" began life as a spiritual sung in Black churches along the Gulf Coast sometime after the 1900 Galveston hurricane, passed along informally before it was ever written down or recorded.

The song's first recording came in 1934, when a preacher known as "Sin-Killer" Griffin performed it for the Library of Congress. It resurfaced decades later in the 1960s folk revival through recordings by Eric Von Schmidt and Tom Rush, and it kept traveling forward from there: James Taylor covered it again as recently as 2009.

Few disaster songs anywhere on this list have as clean and traceable a chain of transmission: over more than a century, the same song about the same 1900 storm moved from oral church tradition to federal archive to folk revival to a contemporary pop artist's catalog, without ever losing its identity as a song specifically about Galveston.

**Johnstown flood, Pennsylvania, 1889.** Sheet music titled "The Johnstown Flood," with words and music by Joseph Flynn, was copyrighted in 1889, the same year as the disaster itself and at a moment before recorded sound was even available in American homes, meaning the song's original life was entirely as printed sheet music performed live.

The Johnstown Flood Museum now maintains a standing gallery devoted specifically to the music the flood inspired, an actual institution built around the premise that this one disaster kept generating art well beyond Flynn's original composition.

**Mount Tambora, 1815, and the 1816 "Year Without a Summer."** Mount Tambora's 1815 eruption triggered the "Year Without a Summer" in 1816, a volcanic winter of endless cold and rain across Europe. Trapped indoors by that weather at a rented villa on Lake Geneva, Mary Shelley and Lord Byron each began work on one of the era's defining pieces of literature within the same few weeks.

Byron wrote the poem "Darkness" and Shelley began *Frankenstein*, both conceived in June or July of 1816, independently of each other but under the identical set of conditions: a volcanic eruption on the other side of the world had darkened the sky enough to keep two of the era's most significant writers indoors together long enough to start writing.

Almost nothing else on this list has as strong a claim to still being alive in culture two hundred years later. *Frankenstein* alone has generated a constant stream of adaptations ever since, meaning a single volcanic winter is still, indirectly, generating new cultural work in the present day.

**Dust Bowl, US Great Plains, 1930s.** The Dust Bowl of the 1930s produced responses across nearly every medium at once. Woody Guthrie released *Dust Bowl Ballads* in 1940, a full album of songs written directly out of the migration and hardship the drought and dust storms caused.

John Steinbeck's *The Grapes of Wrath*, published in 1939, won the Pulitzer Prize and was adapted the following year into John Ford's 1940 film, carrying the same story from novel to screen within twelve months. Dorothea Lange's 1936 photograph "Migrant Mother" became one of the most reproduced images in the entire history of American photography, a single frame that came to stand in for the whole crisis.

Already flagged as a candidate place elsewhere, but the specific artifacts are worth naming directly here: music, literature, film, and photography all produced lasting, still-circulated work out of the same disaster within the same handful of years, making the Dust Bowl probably the single strongest case for showing the entire art and culture spectrum responding to one event.

**Bhopal gas disaster, India, 1984.** Indra Sinha's novel *Animal's People*, published in 2007, is narrated by a man physically disabled by the Bhopal gas leak, telling the story of the disaster's aftermath through a character whose body was permanently altered by it.

The novel was shortlisted for the Booker Prize, placing it among the most recognized literary fiction of its year. Bhopal is an industrial rather than a natural disaster, so this case may sit slightly outside the usual frame of this research, but it is worth including given how directly the fiction traces back to a single, well-documented event.

**Chernobyl disaster, Ukraine (then USSR), 1986.** Svetlana Alexievich's *Voices from Chernobyl*, also translated as *Chernobyl Prayer*, was published in 1997 and compiles oral testimony gathered from survivors and emergency responders who lived through the disaster and its aftermath.

Alexievich won the Nobel Prize in Literature in 2015, and this book was specifically cited as central to that recognition, meaning a work built entirely out of first-person Chernobyl testimony helped earn its author literature's highest honor.

As with Bhopal, Chernobyl is more a contamination disaster than a natural hazard, so it is a slightly different animal than most of the other cases here. But the bar for proof that a work has entered lasting cultural memory is about as high as it gets when the book in question is cited by name in a Nobel citation.

**Great Famine, Ireland, 1845-52.** "Fields of Athenry" was written by Pete St John in 1979, set during the Great Famine more than a century after it ended, telling the story of a fictional man transported for stealing food to feed his starving family.

Despite that distance from the event, the song has become one of the most-performed disaster-adjacent songs in the world today, sung by tens of thousands of people at Irish sporting events on a regular basis.

It stands as a clear example of a culture writing its disaster song generations after the fact rather than in the immediate aftermath, proof that new cultural memory can still take hold widely long after the people who lived through the actual event are gone.
