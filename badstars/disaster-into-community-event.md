# When a disaster becomes a community gathering

## Outline

- The Qingming Water-Releasing Festival (Dujiangyan, Sichuan, China, ongoing since 256 BCE): the oldest entry in this research, marking the still-functioning ancient irrigation system built to control the Min River's flooding.
- Wafaa El-Nil (Egypt, ancient origins, still observed): a two-week festival descended from millennia of measuring and celebrating the Nile's annual flood.
- The Feast of San Gennaro's blood miracle (Naples, Italy, first recorded 1389): a ritual in which the liquefaction of the saint's dried blood is read as an omen for Vesuvius staying quiet.
- Þjóðhátíð (Heimaey, Iceland, founded 1874, survived 1973): an annual festival that continued the same year the Eldfell volcano erupted and buried a third of the town.
- Disaster Prevention Day (Japan, established 1960): a nationwide day of drills held every September 1 to mark the anniversary of the 1923 Great Kantō earthquake.
- The Boston Christmas tree (sent by Nova Scotia, ritual since 1918, revived 1971): an annual gift of thanks for Boston's relief effort after the 1917 Halifax Explosion.
- Katrina second-line parades (New Orleans, since 2006): the city's traditional brass-band parade form used every August 29 to mark the anniversary of the storm.

This is the fourth research pass. It uses two things already in the tool as reference points: Simeulue's smong tradition, which keeps the 1907 tsunami's lesson alive through poems, lullabies, and school curriculum, though the record points to a bundle of ongoing transmission channels rather than one single named annual festival, and Kobe's Luminarie, a purpose-built annual light festival for the Great Hanshin earthquake. The throughline for this list is a specific disaster that a community turned into a recurring, scheduled, public gathering, rather than just a private or one-off memorial, pushed back as far as the record allows, the same approach used in the other two documents.

**The Qingming Water-Releasing Festival, Dujiangyan, Sichuan, China, ongoing since 256 BCE.** This is the oldest entry across all three of these documents by a wide margin. Li Bing, governor of Shu, built the Dujiangyan irrigation system in 256 BCE specifically to control the Min River's flooding while still irrigating the Chengdu Plain.

The system is still functioning today, unmodernized in its basic design, more than 2,200 years later, and is recognized as a UNESCO World Heritage site. Erwang Temple was built to honor Li Bing and his son and has hosted continuous worship for that entire span of time.

Today the site still holds an annual Qingming Water-Releasing Festival, along with a separate temple fair marking the two men's birthdays. It is about as direct a line as exists anywhere in this research between an ancient flood-control engineering project and a still-living, still-scheduled annual public event.

**Wafaa El-Nil, Egypt, ancient origins, still observed.** Before the Aswan Dam was built, the Nile's annual flood was the difference between abundance and famine for Egypt, depending on its exact height, which was measured against nilometers, purpose-built gauges, some of which still stand today, like the 9th-century Rhoda Island nilometer in Cairo.

Pharaonic Egypt personified that flood as the god Hapi, tying the river's rise directly to religious practice rather than treating it as a purely practical measurement.

The modern version of the celebration, Wafaa El-Nil ("Fidelity of the Nile"), is a two-week festival starting August 15 with parades, boat races, and folklore performances, a direct, name-preserved descendant of a ritual thousands of years old, marking a flood that was simultaneously life-giving and, if it came in too short or too great, catastrophic.

**The Feast of San Gennaro's blood miracle, Naples, Italy, first recorded 1389, ongoing.** The blood miracle of San Gennaro was first recorded in 1389 and is still observed today. San Gennaro is the city's patron saint and is specifically invoked for protection from Vesuvius.

Several times a year, including on his September 19 feast day, a reliquary said to hold the saint's dried blood is exposed at the Naples cathedral. A liquefaction of the blood is treated as a good omen for the city and for the volcano staying quiet, while its absence has historically been read as a warning.

The tradition is worth flagging as folk ritual and religious practice first, and disaster-response tradition second, but the two are explicitly and publicly linked in how Neapolitans talk about the ceremony, a centuries-old ritual whose entire meaning is bound up with whether Vesuvius stays dormant.

**Þjóðhátíð, Heimaey (Westman Islands), Iceland, founded 1874, survived 1973.** Þjóðhátíð, the annual festival on Heimaey, was founded in 1874 for reasons unrelated to any disaster: bad weather stopped islanders from reaching the mainland's millennial celebration that year, so they threw their own festival instead.

The disaster relevance came nearly a century later: when the Eldfell volcano erupted without warning in January 1973 and buried a third of the town, forcing a full evacuation of the island, the festival still went ahead that same year once the eruption had ended.

Þjóðhátíð remains one of Iceland's biggest annual gatherings today. The Eldheimar museum, built around one excavated, buried house, now anchors the eruption's memory for the rest of the calendar year, while the festival remains the moment the town still gathers together in person.

**Disaster Prevention Day, Japan, established 1960, observed every September 1st.** Disaster Prevention Day marks the anniversary of the 1923 Great Kantō earthquake, which killed more than 105,000 people, more of them from the fires that followed the quake than from the shaking itself.

The whole country runs disaster drills during that week: schools, offices, and neighborhoods rehearse evacuations, and public exhibitions and lectures are held nationwide.

It stands as one of the few entries in this research where an entire nation's civil-defense calendar is built around a single historical disaster's exact date, turning one catastrophic day in 1923 into a permanent annual fixture of national planning.

**The Boston Christmas tree, sent by Nova Scotia, ritual since 1918, revived 1971.** This is already flagged in the culture document, but it is worth repeating here specifically as a recurring event rather than an object. Nova Scotia has sent Boston an official Christmas tree nearly every year since 1918, the year after the 1917 Halifax Explosion, in thanks for the relief effort Boston sent north in the disaster's immediate aftermath.

The tradition was revived as a deliberate annual practice in 1971 and formalized by the provincial government in 1976, turning an informal gesture of thanks into a standing intergovernmental ritual.

It is a rare example in this research of a community event built around gratitude directed outward, toward the place that helped, rather than a memorial turned inward toward the community's own loss.

**Katrina second-line parades and the annual "Katrina-versary," New Orleans, USA, since 2006.** New Orleans's own brass-band parade tradition, the second line, gets used every year around August 29 for organized remembrance walks and parades through the neighborhoods hit hardest by the storm, most consistently in the Lower Ninth Ward.

The second line is a centuries-old local ritual form used traditionally for both funerals and celebrations, and the annual commemoration folds directly onto that existing form rather than inventing a new kind of ceremony from scratch.

The tradition has continued since 2006, giving New Orleans a recurring, scheduled public gathering that channels the anniversary of the storm through a ritual practice the city had already been using for exactly this kind of collective mourning and celebration for generations.
