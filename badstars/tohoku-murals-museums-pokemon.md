# Three more threads for the 2011 Japan/Tohoku case

Meant to fold directly into the tool's existing Tohoku material (currently the dimmed `tohoku` stub, cross-referenced from the live Aneyoshi tsunami-stone case), not stand alone. All three sit in genuine tension with each other in a way that's worth keeping intact rather than smoothing over: the same disaster produced both a landscape of permanent absence and a landscape of deliberately cheerful, almost cartoonish presence, often within a few miles of each other.

## Outline

- **Murals in towns still full of permanently empty houses:** street artists in Onagawa, Futaba, and elsewhere painted brightly colored murals directly onto a townscape still working through demolition, some cheerful and some elegiac, in the years after the tsunami and the Fukushima evacuation.
- **Wrecked schools deliberately preserved and turned into museums:** Okawa Elementary in Ishinomaki and Arahama Elementary in Sendai, where dozens died and hundreds sheltered respectively, were kept in their damaged states rather than demolished, to physically transmit the scale of the disaster to people who didn't live through it.
- **Pokémon Local Acts, and Lapras specifically, Miyagi Prefecture:** The Pokémon Company formalized a recovery partnership assigning each Tohoku prefecture its own ambassador Pokémon, with Miyagi's Lapras growing from a 2016 tourism event into a full theme park by 2025, aimed at giving children something cheerful to rally around.

## Murals in towns still full of permanently empty houses

In Onagawa, one of the hardest-hit towns on the entire coast, with more than 70% of homes and buildings destroyed and close to 10% of the population dead or missing, a street artist known as D-Bons spent years painting the fishermen's storage containers in the main port and surrounding villages with brightly colored animals and lettering. The color was deliberately placed against a townscape still working through demolition and rebuilding, a visual counterweight to a place that had lost most of its physical fabric in a single afternoon.

In Futaba, one of the towns closest to the Fukushima Daiichi plant and evacuated far longer than the tsunami-only towns (it didn't reopen to visitors until March 2020), the art company Over Alls painted a series of street murals specifically meant, in their own framing, to lighten the mood and pay tribute to those affected. These murals went up on a townscape that was still functionally a ghost town at the time, applied to streets with almost no residents yet to see them in person, an act of faith in a future audience more than a response to a present one.

Separately, the artist Hiroshige Kagawa took the opposite approach: large-scale watercolor murals, some roughly 20 by 50 feet, that don't brighten the ruin but depict it directly, abandoned buildings and windswept fields rendered as surreal, elegiac landscape painting rather than public cheer. Both approaches are worth keeping in the case side by side, murals that argue against the ruin and murals that sit with it, since together they show the same disaster generating two opposite instincts about what public art after catastrophe should even try to do.

## Wrecked schools deliberately preserved and turned into museums

Two sites anchor this thread, and both are worth including since they represent slightly different choices about how much to preserve. Okawa Elementary School, in Ishinomaki, sat 3.7 kilometers inland from the coast and was still hit by a wave 8.6 meters high; 74 students and 10 teachers died there, part of 416 deaths in the immediate area. The school building has been deliberately maintained with minimal alteration and opened to the public since July 2021, alongside an adjacent Okawa Memorial Museum built specifically to hold photographs and materials from before and after the disaster, pairing the raw structure with curated documentation rather than leaving visitors to interpret the ruin on its own.

Arahama Elementary School, in Sendai, sheltered 320 people who survived by evacuating to its upper floors while the tsunami reached the second story below them. It has been kept in its damaged state for the same explicit reason as Okawa: to transmit the physical scale of the threat to people who didn't live through it.

Functionally, both schools operate as hazard markers not unlike the tsunami stones scattered across the region, just built at the scale of an entire building instead of a single carved rock. Where a stone carries a warning in words, these buildings carry the warning in structural damage itself, a wave line visible on a wall, a stairwell that people actually climbed to survive, evidence that doesn't require translation to be understood.

## Pokémon Local Acts, and Lapras specifically, Miyagi Prefecture

The strangest and most distinctly modern entry in this whole research corpus, and worth taking seriously rather than treating as a novelty. The Pokémon Company's Tohoku recovery involvement started informally in the years right after 2011 and formalized in 2018 as Pokémon Local Acts, assigning each participating Tohoku prefecture its own "ambassador" Pokémon chosen for a pun or thematic connection to the region: Geodude for Iwate, Lapras for Miyagi, Chansey for Fukushima.

Miyagi's Lapras partnership started concretely in 2016 through a Pokémon GO recovery event in Ishinomaki, became the prefecture's official ambassador role in 2019, and has since grown into physical, permanent installations. These include a dedicated Lapras Park, opened 2025 inside Moririn Kasenuma Park in the town of Rifu, built around a larger-than-life Lapras statue, alongside smaller touches like a Lapras-shaped snow tube for kids.

The explicit intent, stated by both the company and local tourism boards, was to give children specifically something upbeat and communal to rally around in the recovery years. This is not a memorial in the plaque-or-museum sense; it is closer to a mascot chosen deliberately to make a devastated place feel like somewhere worth playing again, a strategy aimed less at commemorating what happened than at giving the youngest survivors a reason to look forward to something.

## Why this belongs as one entry, not three

Read together, this is an unusual three-part Tohoku story: murals responding to a landscape of permanent loss, museums built from the wreckage itself to make sure the loss isn't misremembered as smaller than it was, and a state-sanctioned cartoon mascot built specifically to give the youngest survivors something cheerful to hold onto in the meantime. If the tool ever promotes the Tohoku stub to a full case, this is probably the richest possible three-stranded version of it, arguably a case study in how a single community can run Strengthening (murals, memory-work) and something closer to Formulation-through-play (the Pokémon material) side by side, rather than moving through the tool's stages in strict sequence.
