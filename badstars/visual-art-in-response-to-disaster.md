# Visual art made in response to disasters

Third research pass, narrowed to visual art specifically: painting, photography, sculpture, installation, mural, print. Split the same way as the culture doc: what's already in the tool, what's sitting in the thesis unused, and new candidates from outside research. It mixes old and folk work with contemporary work throughout, since the strongest examples span both, and adds a fourth section for the deep-time round: pushing this specific question, visual art made in response to disaster, back as far as the record allows, well before anything in the tool or the earlier lists.

## Outline

**Already in the thesis**
- **Ai Weiwei, "Remembering" (2009):** A facade installation of 9,000 children's backpacks spelling out a bereaved mother's words for her child, made after the 2008 Sichuan earthquake, that led to Ai Weiwei's investigation and censorship by the Chinese government.

**New finds worth considering**
- **Arnold Genthe, "Looking Down Sacramento Street" (1906):** A photograph shot on borrowed equipment hours after the San Francisco earthquake destroyed the photographer's own studio.
- **Dorothea Lange's "Migrant Mother" and Arthur Rothstein's "Fleeing a Dust Storm" (1936):** Two of the most reproduced Dust Bowl photographs in American history, one of them a partly staged image.
- **John Steuart Curry, "Tornado over Kansas" (1929):** A Regionalist painting of a family racing a funnel cloud that Curry's own fellow Kansans reportedly resented as their state's defining image.

## Already in the thesis, and it was missed the first pass

This material was sitting directly in the thesis's own introduction (pages 9-12) and wasn't pulled into either of the first two research documents. Correcting that here, since these examples are stronger and more specifically anchored to the thesis than most of what turned up in outside research.

**Ai Weiwei, "Remembering" (2009), Haus der Kunst, Munich.** This is a facade installation of 9,000 children's backpacks in five bright colors, arranged to spell out a sentence in Mandarin: "She lived happily for seven years in this world." The line comes from a mother of one of the more than 5,000 schoolchildren killed in the 2008 Sichuan earthquake, most of them in schools built with substandard "tofu-dreg" construction that collapsed while other buildings nearby survived.

Ai Weiwei was investigated and repeatedly censored by the Chinese government specifically for compiling and publishing the names of the earthquake's child victims, a project the state treated as politically dangerous in its own right. The backpack installation grew directly out of that same effort to name the dead.

It is about as direct a link as exists anywhere between a specific disaster, a specific artwork, and real state retaliation over the act of remembering itself. The backpacks are not an abstract symbol of loss; they are built from the same information, a literal list of children, that got the artist investigated for compiling it.

**Yasushi Ebihara, "Afternoon," part of the exhibition "2:46 and Thereafter."** This piece is cited directly in the thesis. The exhibition's title refers to 2:46 PM, the exact time the March 11, 2011 tsunami hit Japan, naming the whole show after the precise minute a community's "after" began. The specificity of that framing, down to the minute rather than the day, is itself the point: it fixes the exhibition to the exact moment the disaster imposed a before-and-after on everyone connected to it.

**"no existe un mundo poshuracán: Puerto Rican Art in the Wake of Hurricane Maria," Whitney Museum of American Art (Nov 2022-Apr 2023).** This exhibition is also in the thesis, cited from a 2022 ArtNews piece by Taylor Dafoe. Per the thesis's own notes, the show's stated goal was explicitly not another celebration of resilience-in-tragedy, but an attempt to show the cracks Maria opened in Puerto Rico's politics, culture, and colonial status.

The standout piece is Gabriella Torres-Ferrer's "Untitled (Valora tu mentira americana)" (2018): an actual fallen utility post pulled from Maria's wreckage, hung diagonally from the ceiling, still bearing a pre-storm statehood-campaign sign that read "Value your American citizenship, guarantee it, vote for statehood." The artwork's title translates that same sign's message into "Value your American lie," turning a piece of storm debris and a piece of pre-storm political messaging into a single object.

The piece is now in the Whitney's permanent collection, which means a fallen utility pole carrying a pre-Maria political slogan has become part of a major American museum's holdings, a physical fragment of the storm's aftermath that continues to make its political argument every time it is shown.

**"RicanStruction: Reminiscing & Rebuilding Puerto Rico," comic anthology.** Distinct from the Whitney show, this is Edgardo Miranda-Rodríguez's benefit anthology comic starring his own character La Borinqueña, named for the Taíno name for Puerto Rico, teamed up with DC and Marvel heroes like Superman, Batman, and Wonder Woman. It was published specifically to raise relief money after Hurricane Maria, a case of mainstream superhero iconography being redirected toward direct disaster fundraising rather than commemoration alone.

**O, Miami, "Poems to the Sky" project, Miami International Airport.** This is a typographic mural built from a line written by Nieema Marshal, then a third grader, as part of a poetry workshop the O, Miami festival ran for elementary schoolers about growing up in South Florida's climate. Her line, "I am from a place where it does not snow," hangs in full view of every plane arriving at MIA.

The piece is small in scale, but it is a literal, permanent, public installation of a child's own words about the place. It was already cited once in this research's hurricane-culture material as a source, and it is worth surfacing here as an actual visual artifact in its own right rather than just a citation.

**Jorna Newberry's "Fire Dreaming" (Waru Tjukurrpa) paintings.** These are already pulled and live in the tool, but worth restating for the record. Newberry is a Pitjantjatjara artist whose Dreaming paintings depict controlled burns in the abstract style she learned from her uncle, the well-known Aboriginal artist Tommy Watson, tied to her mother's country at Utantja on the WA/NT/SA border.

The work is a contemporary continuation of what the thesis clocks as a roughly 40,000-year Aboriginal tradition of fire as both hunting tool and land management, not a modern reinvention of that tradition. The paintings carry forward, in a living artistic practice, a hazard-management technology that predates almost everything else in this research by tens of thousands of years.

## New finds worth considering

**Arnold Genthe, "Looking Down Sacramento Street, San Francisco, April 18, 1906."** Genthe's own studio and cameras were destroyed in the earthquake that morning, so he borrowed a handheld camera and shot the city anyway. The resulting photograph shows residents standing calmly in the street as the fire's smoke rolls in from Nob Hill in the background.

This single image is now individually famous enough to have its own Wikipedia page and a home in the Library of Congress. Since the tool's existing sf-1906 case is currently a dimmed, thin entry, this photograph could serve as the anchor image if that case is ever promoted to a full one.

**Dorothea Lange, "Migrant Mother" (1936), and Arthur Rothstein, "Fleeing a Dust Storm" (1936).** These are two of the most reproduced photographs in American history, both produced through the Farm Security Administration's Dust Bowl documentation project. Rothstein's image, a farmer and his two sons walking into a wall of blowing dust toward a half-buried shed, was staged to some degree, a minor controversy at the time, which is itself an interesting wrinkle for research interested in how disaster imagery gets made and remembered rather than simply captured.

**John Steuart Curry, "Tornado over Kansas" (1929).** This is a Kansas-born Regionalist painter's depiction of a farm family racing a funnel cloud to their storm cellar, partly guided by newly-taken photographs of a real 1929 tornado near Hardtner, Kansas. It won second prize at the 1933 Carnegie International, was reproduced in full color in Time in 1934, and is now called a "national treasure" by the museum that holds it.

Notably, Curry's fellow Kansans reportedly disliked him picking a tornado as the state's defining image, its own small case study in a community not wanting its disaster commemorated even when the resulting artwork became nationally celebrated.

**The Katrina "X-codes," New Orleans, 2005-present.** Search-and-rescue teams spray-painted a standardized X on every building they cleared after Katrina, recording the date, the search team, and the body count in each quadrant of the mark. Thousands of homeowners chose to preserve the mark rather than paint over it, and some had it cast in permanent metal.

A dedicated retrospective, "Katrina + 5: An X-Code Exhibition" by Dorothy Moye, treats the marks as a visual language in their own right, worth studying the way any other typographic or symbolic system would be studied. Tattoo parlors across New Orleans still ink X-codes onto survivors and first responders as a permanent, literal marker of the storm, decades after the marks were first sprayed.

This is a rare example of official disaster bureaucracy's own visual notation, something created purely as an administrative record, becoming folk art through the choice of the people it was made about.

**Munemasa Takahashi, "Lost & Found Project," and the broader tsunami photo-restoration effort, Japan, 2011-2015.** After the Tōhoku tsunami, volunteers began washing mud off family photographs pulled from the wreckage by hand at evacuation shelters. The effort scaled into organized projects, including Ricoh's "Save the Memory" and the volunteer-run "Memory Salvage Project," that eventually cleaned and returned several hundred thousand photographs to survivors.

Takahashi's Lost & Found Project turned the restoration work itself into a photography project and exhibition, a case where the recovery of ordinary family snapshots became its own act of public art, rather than the photographs simply being returned quietly to their owners.

**Kobe Luminarie, Japan, annual since 1995.** This is a large-scale light installation held every year to commemorate the roughly 6,000 people killed in the Great Hanshin earthquake, and it uses electricity as its own symbol: the quake had knocked out power across the city, so the annual display of over 200,000 hand-painted lights functions explicitly as a statement of recovery rather than just decoration.

The event draws three to five million visitors most years, with a silent commemoration and reading of victims' names built into the opening ceremony. It sits closer to public installation art than to a gallery piece, but it is a massive, sustained one, repeated annually for decades.

## Going back further: deep-time visual art

**Akrotiri frescoes, Thera (modern Santorini), Greece: c. 1627 BCE.** The Bronze Age Aegean town of Akrotiri was buried under meters of pumice when Thera erupted, in one of the largest eruptions of the last 10,000 years, and that same ash is exactly what preserved the wall paintings inside nearly every excavated house: religious processions, ships, animals, everyday life, some of the earliest surviving examples of Aegean art at all.

A meaningful twist for this research's own themes is that no human remains have been found at the site, and the layout suggests residents had time to evacuate, likely after a preceding earthquake gave warning. That makes Akrotiri a rare ancient case where the formulation-and-response cycle actually seems to have worked, just before the disaster that ended up preserving its art for thousands of years.

The result is a strange inversion: the same eruption that ended the town's life as a living place is the reason its art survived at all, sealed under ash rather than lost to time, weather, or reuse the way most Bronze Age painting has been.

**The Aztec Sun Stone (Piedra del Sol), Mexico: early 16th century, pre-conquest.** This 24-ton carved basalt monument, now in Mexico City's National Museum of Anthropology, is generally read as a depiction of the Aztec Five Suns cosmology: a cycle of four prior worlds, each destroyed in turn by jaguars, hurricanes, a rain of fire, and finally a flood that turned humanity into fish, before arriving at the current, fifth sun.

The myth itself survives mainly through the colonial-era Codex Chimalpopoca, written in 1558, but the stone is the physical, monumental version of the same disaster-cycle cosmology, carved centuries earlier than that written account. It is worth pairing with Ma Yuan's Yellow River paintings, covered elsewhere in this research, as another culture treating catastrophic flood as a defining, recurring feature of cosmic time rather than a one-off event.

**The Pompeii body casts, Italy (disaster 79 CE, technique invented 1863).** These are not paintings, but they are arguably the most viscerally famous disaster imagery in the world. The archaeologist Giuseppe Fiorelli realized that decomposed Vesuvius victims had left voids in the hardened ash around their bodies, and began pouring plaster into those cavities to recover the exact final poses of the dead.

The casts are still made today, with resin having mostly replaced plaster, and they are still displayed at the site and still among the most reproduced disaster images anywhere in the world. It is a technique for turning absence itself, the literal empty space left by a vanished body, into a permanent visual artifact.

**The Gilgamesh flood tablet, Mesopotamia (composed centuries earlier, tablet copied 7th century BCE).** This is covered in more depth in the events and monuments documents elsewhere in this research, but it is worth a note here too. The cuneiform tablet itself, discovered in the ruins of Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh and deciphered by George Smith in 1872, is a physical object as well as a text.

Depictions of Utnapishtim's boat and the flood have been a recurring subject in art and illustration ever since the story resurfaced in the 1870s, meaning a Bronze Age flood narrative has continued generating new visual work for a century and a half after its rediscovery.
