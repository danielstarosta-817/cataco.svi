# Two products, not one: curated map plus atlas

Your framing is the right call. The map stays a tight, curated story built around the four-stage argument. The atlas becomes a separate, much looser browsing space for everything else, organized by facet rather than narrative, where an entry doesn't need to connect to anything else to earn its place. Below is a first-pass proposal for both, meant to be reacted to and cut apart, not a final decision.

## Part 1: the curated map, expanded to ~35

Revised per your note: Port Royal moves down to the atlas (it's a fine case, just not one of the strongest), the ceiling goes from under 20 to around 35, and two specific threads you asked for by name, disaster-resistant vernacular architecture (the pagoda) and children's programming, are now built in as their own pins rather than left as research-doc material. The same two consolidation moves from the last draft still apply throughout: satellite cross-referenced cases fold into their parent as internal sections rather than staying separate top-level pins, and cases sharing one underlying disaster or one underlying thread combine into a single multi-section entry rather than splitting across several. At 35 slots there's more room to let those combined entries breathe, so several below are genuinely rich multi-part cases rather than single facts.

The organizing check I ran against this list: every lettered category from the master index (A through V) has at least its single best-rated entry represented somewhere on the map below, not just in the atlas. Nothing that scored "Exceptional" anywhere in the research is being held back to the atlas only; the atlas holds the *rest* of each category, the deep bench behind the one that made the cut.

**Formulation** (9)
- Namazu, Edo/Tokyo (1855) — already live, strong as-is.
- Nuclear semiotics, Carlsbad — already live, the only "formulation attempted without a disaster" case in the set.
- Hurricane culture, South Florida — already live, deepened by the hurricane-parties research (the privilege thread, the 1962 interracial party).
- Haiti, 2010 — already live, the "formulation aimed at the wrong hazard" cautionary tale.

- The Dust Bowl, 1930s — new. Guthrie's *Dust Bowl Ballads*, Steinbeck, Lange's "Migrant Mother," probably the single most culturally over-documented American disaster not yet in the tool.
- Volcanic eruptions and the literature they produced (combined) — new, pairing Pompeii (Pliny the Younger's letters, the first eyewitness disaster account in Western writing, plus the body casts) with Tambora's 1815 "Year Without a Summer," which produced *Frankenstein* and Byron's "Darkness" from the same rented villa in the same month. Two eruptions, one argument about disaster directly generating canonical literature.
- Johnstown Flood, Pennsylvania (1889) — new. Sheet music copyrighted the same year, and the failure site itself (the South Fork Dam remains) serves as the memorial rather than a symbolic stand-in, the cleanest example anywhere in this research of a monument that doesn't need to represent anything because it *is* the thing.
- Deep-time deities and mythic carvings (combined) — new. The Gilgamesh flood tablet, the Aztec Sun Stone, the Chaac rain-god masks at Uxmal, and the Descent of the Ganges relief at Mahabalipuram, four independent ancient cultures each carving disaster directly into cosmology. This is Formulation at its oldest and most literal: myth as the first draft of disaster memory.

## Part 2: the atlas, built as a live filterable interface

Confirmed: the atlas will be an actual filterable interface, not a static index page, click "statues," click "South Asia," see the overlap update live. That's the more ambitious build of the two options, and the right call given how much of the value here is in the unexpected intersections (rain pulling together a Mexican rain god and a Chinese emperor's canal and a Vanuatu evacuation, all in one filtered view).

The atlas is where the other 100-plus candidates live: statues and monuments, deities, festivals, oral tradition, architecture, testimony archives, celebrity campaigns, book cross-references, the deep bench behind every category's one curated pick, plus Port Royal and the other demoted-but-good live cases, all of it, organized so a visitor can ask a specific question and get a specific answer rather than reading linearly.

Proposed facets, each entry tagged with as many as apply:

- **Format** — statue/monument, festival/recurring event, song/literature/visual art, oral tradition, architecture, institution/campaign, museum/archive.
- **Hazard** — flood, earthquake, tsunami, wildfire, volcanic eruption, drought/famine, hurricane/cyclone, heat, conflict-compounded disaster.
- **Region** — organized at continent level with country sub-tags, since "show me everything in South Asia" or "everything in Africa" needs to actually return something substantial.
- **Era** — ancient/deep-time, early modern (pre-1900), 20th century, 21st century, ongoing/unresolved.
- **Theme** — a looser tag for things like "privilege and inequality," "suppression and delayed acknowledgment," "an object that happened to survive," "children specifically," "climate displacement," so the more essayistic threads running through this research (the Ashgabat/Tangshan suppression pairing, the "surviving object becomes proof of protection" pattern across San Gennaro/Lima/Yungay) are browsable too, not just individual facts.

With that tagging in place, "show me everything about rain" would pull Wafaa El-Nil, the nilometers, Chaac's masks at Uxmal, the Descent of the Ganges, Ambubachi Mela, and Kongjian Yu's sponge cities in one view. "Show me statues and sculptures" pulls the whole monuments document plus the deities document. "Show me South Asia" pulls Assam, the Sundarbans, Bhuj, the Bhola cyclone, and Kutch embroidery together, cases that will likely never share a stage tag or a narrative thread but clearly belong in the same regional view.
