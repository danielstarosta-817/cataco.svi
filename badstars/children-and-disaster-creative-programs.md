## The full entries

### The Flying Seagull Project

Founded in 2008 by entertainer Ash Perrin, the Flying Seagull Project brings circus performers, clowns, and simple play, games, bubbles, balloons, music, dancing, to children in crisis: refugee camps, hospitals, disaster zones, slums. The model is deliberately unclinical. Nobody on the team is running a therapy session in the formal sense; they are running a circus show, and the processing happens as a byproduct of being allowed to be a child again for an afternoon.

They've worked in the Calais "Jungle," calling it one of the hardest environments they've ever worked in, and in the Lesbos camps through their "Love on Lesbos" project, reportedly reaching more than 130,000 children worldwide across these and other deployments. That range, from a French port-town camp to Greek island reception centers, points to a program built to travel to wherever displaced children currently are rather than one tied to a single disaster or a single country's recovery infrastructure.

The organization's own framing is worth keeping close to the record: kids who arrive unable or unwilling to speak leave the sessions vocal and engaged, not because anyone processed trauma with them directly, but because someone gave them permission to just play again. That is a direct, non-clinical counterpart to the more formal art-therapy material already in the tool, and it makes an explicit claim that recovery doesn't require a diagnosis or a trained therapist in the room, only a reliable, repeated invitation to be playful in a place that has stopped being safe for that.

### Za'atari refugee camp, Jordan, skateboarding and the Makani centers

Make Life Skate Life, a nonprofit that builds skateparks specifically in crisis zones, partnered with the NGO ACTED to teach Syrian refugee children in Za'atari to skateboard. Kids were also bused weekly to 7Hills, a purpose-built community skatepark in Amman built for youth from difficult circumstances, extending the program beyond the camp's own boundaries and giving children a reason to leave the camp for something other than an emergency.

Inside the camp itself, thirteen "Makani" centers combine drawing, dancing, and play with actual psychological support for children processing displacement and loss. That combination, informal creative activity sitting inside the same physical space as formal psychological care, means the two approaches aren't competing for the same children's attention; they're running side by side under one roof.

Taken together, the skateboarding and the Makani centers amount to a sport-based version of the same basic idea behind the Flying Seagull Project: physical play as its own form of processing, running in parallel with, rather than instead of, more formal support. The specific choice of skateboarding, a solitary, physically demanding, falling-and-getting-back-up activity, is a notably different register from circus performance or drawing, but it is aimed at the same underlying goal of giving displaced children something to master that has nothing to do with the disaster that displaced them.

### Already in the tool, worth restating in this specific frame

The `peregian` case (2019 Queensland bushfires) and the transcript reference to post-1989 Loma Prieta earthquake classrooms both describe teachers using art projects, drawing, painting, simple creative tasks, to give children who don't yet have the words for what they lived through a different channel to process it. The `creative-arts` case (post-Katrina New Orleans) covers the same idea at a slightly older age range and with more formal art-therapy framing.

Worth keeping all of these visually and narratively linked to the newer, less clinical examples above, since the throughline, kids don't need a diagnosis to benefit from being handed a crayon, a costume, or a skateboard, is the same one running through all of them. The variation across the set, formal art therapy in New Orleans, classroom drawing exercises after Loma Prieta and in Peregian, unstructured circus play with the Flying Seagull Project, and skateboarding in Za'atari, maps a spectrum from clinical to purely playful, all converging on the same basic mechanism of giving children a non-verbal outlet.
