# "Half a good house": incomplete-by-design and participatory recovery housing

Worth separating two things that get conflated here: the original Elemental project was a poverty-housing project, not strictly a disaster-recovery one, but the same firm immediately reused the same logic for an actual post-earthquake rebuild, and the underlying idea, build less so residents finish the rest themselves, on their own resources and timeline, so the result feels authored rather than issued, shows up independently in at least two other disaster contexts below.

## In this document

- **Quinta Monroy, Iquique, Chile (completed 2004):** Architect Alejandro Aravena and his firm Elemental used Chile's standard housing subsidy to build only "half a good house" for 100 squatting families, leaving the rest for residents to finish themselves.
- **Constitución, Chile (2010 earthquake and tsunami recovery):** Elemental took the same incremental-housing logic directly into disaster reconstruction, pairing it with a coastal forest planted as a tsunami buffer.
- **Barefoot Social Architecture, Sindh Province, Pakistan (Yasmeen Lari, active since the 2010 and 2022 floods):** Architect Yasmeen Lari's low-cost bamboo, mud, and lime shelters are complete structures, but built entirely by flood survivors themselves, with a public goal of one million units after the 2022 floods.

## Quinta Monroy, Iquique, Chile

In 2004, architect Alejandro Aravena and his firm Elemental completed Quinta Monroy, the origin case for this entire idea. They were given Chile's standard low-income housing subsidy, about $7,500 per family at the time, to resettle roughly 100 families who had been squatting the same downtown site for three decades. A conventional developer working from that same budget would have built small, complete houses far outside the city, which was the usual solution at that price point, pushing poor families out to the periphery, away from the jobs, schools, and transit they already depended on.

Elemental did something different with the money. Rather than spending the full subsidy on finished square footage, the firm spent most of it buying the land itself, so families could stay exactly where they already were, in the center of the city. What they built on that land for each family was only half of a good house: a structurally sound three-story frame that included a kitchen, a bathroom, and a staircase, with the remaining volume left open for families to enclose and finish on their own, as money allowed, over months or years.

Aravena's own framing of the idea, since repeated widely, was that a small complete house is a small complete house forever, but half of a good house, if the unbuilt half is designed properly, can eventually grow into a good house, built with the resident's own money and labor rather than the state's. Photographs taken years later back that up: the units had grown in completely different directions from each other, with individual color choices, added rooms, and even shopfronts, the kind of "feels theirs" outcome that a finished, identical unit handed over complete could never really produce.

## Constitución, Chile

Constitución is the most important follow-on case for this document specifically, because it takes the exact same incremental logic from Quinta Monroy and applies it directly to disaster recovery rather than ordinary poverty housing. After the February 2010 Maule earthquake and tsunami devastated the coastal city of Constitución, Elemental won a public design competition to lead the city's reconstruction plan.

Elemental extended the "half a house" model into the rebuilt residential areas of Constitución, using the same core logic as Quinta Monroy: give displaced families a stable, structurally sound starting point rather than a fully finished unit, and let them complete the rest themselves once they had the resources and time to do it. Applied to an actual post-disaster population rather than a chronically underhoused one, the model carried the same underlying bet, that residents finishing their own homes end up with something that both works and means more to them than a house simply handed over complete.

The Constitución plan also included a separate, widely cited piece of the same reconstruction: a forest planted along the vulnerable coastline specifically as a tsunami buffer, functioning as green infrastructure instead of a seawall. Together, the incremental housing and the buffer forest represent a coherent recovery philosophy: give people a stable, safe starting structure and let them personalize the rest, while handling the shared hazard, the tsunami risk itself, at the landscape scale rather than the individual-house scale.

## Barefoot Social Architecture, Sindh Province, Pakistan

Architect Yasmeen Lari has taken a different route to a related idea. Instead of a half-finished permanent structure, her "Lari Octa Green" shelters are complete, but designed explicitly to be built by flood survivors themselves, in about a week, from bamboo, mud, and lime, at a small fraction of the cost of conventional post-disaster housing. The program has been active since the 2010 floods and expanded sharply after the catastrophic 2022 Pakistan floods.

Lari's own description of the approach, treating "communities as partners, not victims," is close to a direct restatement of the Elemental logic from the opposite direction. Rather than leaving physical space for residents to finish later, the entire process of construction itself is handed to residents up front, so the labor and decision-making that Quinta Monroy left for after move-in happens here from the very first day of building.

Following the 2022 floods, Lari and the Heritage Foundation of Pakistan set a public goal of building one million such shelters, with roughly a third of that target built as of the most recent reporting available. At that scale, the project functions less as a single demonstration house and more as an attempt to make survivor-built, low-cost, locally-sourced shelter the default response to flooding across the province, rather than an exception.

## Why this belongs with the rest of the research

All three cases share the same underlying wager as the smong oral-tradition case and the vernacular-architecture material: that people who were directly involved in building or finishing their own shelter, whether through inherited knowledge, hands-on labor, or literal unfinished physical space left for them to claim, end up with something that both works better and means more than something simply handed to them complete.

It's worth considering whether this becomes its own case, Constitución specifically, since it is a real disaster-recovery project with a specific place, date, and named architect, or a cross-reference thread tying together the Lahaina rebuild questions, the fire-and-land-management "ritual and tool are the same act" pattern, and this participatory-housing material as three versions of one argument about what a good recovery process asks of the people living through it.
