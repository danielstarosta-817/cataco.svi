A wireframe · three places, one idea

Some of the oldest tools for processing a disaster were never meant to be a treatment. They were just something to do with your hands, or a page to write on, while the rest of the world figured out what to do next.

2005 · Hurricane Katrina

New Orleans

A city that drew on tools decades old: a flood song, a spray-painted code, and a culture that refused to wait for permission to start healing.

Louisiana, Louisiana—they're trying to wash us away.Randy Newman, "Louisiana 1927"

Randy Newman wrote that song more than fifty years after the flood it describes. For decades it stayed a period piece. Then Katrina hit, and the city adopted it as its own anthem. New Orleans native Aaron Neville, who'd performed it for years, put the shift plainly: "when I used to sing that song, it was about something that happened a long time ago. Now when I sing it, it's about something that happened to me and my family."

Finding a voice

Katrina's psychological toll kept climbing for years after landfall. Disaster mental health has leaned increasingly on creative arts therapy, precisely because it can meet people where their own cultural vocabulary already is, instead of importing a clinical language that may not translate.

The X on the door

Search-and-rescue teams marked every home they checked with a spray-painted X, a working code, never meant as art. Many homeowners kept it anyway. In Bywater, Erica Larkin Gaudet recast her partner's X into an iron sculpture he called his house's "Katrina Stigmata," a tattoo for having made it. The word residents kept reaching for, on their houses if not their own skin, was tattoo: a mark you don't take off, whether or not you'd have chosen it.

That instinct, giving a whole community a shared, physical way to hold what just happened to it, wasn't unique to New Orleans, or to adults. It would show up again, decades earlier and two thousand miles away, in a room full of ten-year-olds who didn't yet have the vocabulary for what they'd just lived through either.

Three decades before Katrina, a different disaster had already taught a very different room the same lesson.

1989 · Loma Prieta earthquake

Santa Cruz

A poet already embedded in one classroom turned a shaken school year into a published book, and, twenty years later, a reunion.

It really messed us up. I was diving under desks at loud sounds for weeks, and we didn't have school for a month.Gault school student, on the Loma Prieta earthquake

Bay Area poet Patrice Vecchione had already been the Gault school's poet-in-residence for two years before the quake hit, so her students "already had poetry as a practice," and thanks to regular drills, "they already had earthquakes as a practice" too. What changed after Loma Prieta was how she used that existing practice.

A vehicle that was already there

Her core rule: "you are the boss of your poem, you should feel free to change your mind, as often as you change your socks." That gave students permission to approach the quake through whatever reference already felt safe, baseball, Godzilla, an annoying sibling, rather than requiring them to describe the fear directly. It gave them, in her words, "some distance from our experience, and then to look at it in a more stable place."

A few months later, once the poems were collected, the owner of the destroyed local bookstore invited the students to a book-tent reading that drew several hundred people. "The organizing process was healing for the community in the act of rebuilding," Vecchione said, "carried by memories that were lodged in the physical books more than by the words on the page."

Twenty years later, together again

In 2009, on the earthquake's twentieth anniversary, the original students, adults by then, came back to read their own poems aloud again. "There was a lot of crying," one said afterward, "but it wasn't actually a sad or traumatic event, it felt really good and happy actually." A shared piece of language written as ten-year-olds was still doing the same job it always had.

Nothing about that required a published book or a poet-in-residence. That's one thread worth pulling on its own: how far the same basic idea, giving people a wordless way to hold what happened, stretches when nobody's funding it. But Loma Prieta opens onto a second thread too, just as real: what actually happened to the buildings, the codes, the city itself, the next time the ground in a place like this moved.

1985 & 2017 · earthquakes

Mexico City

A city that remembers its worst earthquake vividly, and in some cases still hasn't finished responding to it.

A quake that changed the rules, and one that didn't finish the job

Mexico City's 1985 earthquake killed thousands and triggered a real, lasting overhaul of the city's structural building codes. The memory of it hasn't faded: it shows up again in the aftermath of 2017's earthquake, when the city drew directly on what it had learned three decades earlier.

But commemoration is not support. Even as memorial projects for the 2017 earthquake went up, hundreds of people displaced by 1985 were, as of a few years ago, still living in government camps built to be temporary.

A city can hold a disaster vividly in its public memory while still failing, in very material terms, the people that disaster actually displaced. This branch ends here in the wireframe, but in the real version it would keep going, maybe into 1985's own building-code thread elsewhere, maybe into another city's version of commemoration-without-repair.

2019 · Queensland bushfires

Peregian

No outside therapist, no dedicated curriculum. One elementary school teacher improvised, and it worked anyway.

Many of the children experienced nightmares after the fires, and art sessions were a catalyst for conversations that might not otherwise have happened.Elementary school teacher, Peregian, Queensland, 2019

Children used "swirly patterns" to represent the wildfire itself and colorful scarves to represent the different stages of how they felt, a shared, wordless way into a conversation the class might never have had otherwise.

Meeting kids where they already were

Clinical psychologists describe exactly what this teacher stumbled into on her own: art "can allow that emotional expression, and that then can be something to be talked about," especially for children who don't yet have adult language for fear or loss. Done well, it becomes, in one clinician's words, "a very beautiful way of taking the past that's been destroyed and making something beautiful for the future."

Nothing about Peregian required advance planning or a published outcome. It required one attentive adult and a box of art supplies, the same basic mechanism as a poet-in-residence in Santa Cruz or a spray-painted code in New Orleans, just caught at its smallest, least funded, most improvised scale.

The actual throughline

Three rooms, three decades, three continents. None of them waited for a formal protocol to show up. A song, a poem, a drawing, a mark on a door — the medium changes, the mechanism doesn't.

This is a wireframe: three stops stitched together to test whether the meander itself feels right, before deciding how the rest of the map folds into it.
