It's a cross-reference and survey document rather than a set of deep case studies in its own right: most of the depth here belongs to the handful of candidates flagged as real gaps, while cases already covered elsewhere just get a pointer to where the fuller version lives.

## The Big Ones (Lucy Jones)

The confirmed chapter list: the Pompeii/Vesuvius eruption (79 CE), the 1755 Lisbon earthquake, Iceland's 1783 Laki eruption, California's 1861-62 flooding, the 1923 Tokyo/Great Kantō earthquake, the 1927 Mississippi flood, China's 1976 Tangshan earthquake, the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, 2005's Hurricane Katrina, Italy's 2009 L'Aquila earthquake, and Japan's 2011 earthquake, tsunami, and Fukushima meltdown. Jones's own throughline, stated plainly in the book, is that these are never purely physical events: every one of them is also a story about the human institutions, inequality, and politics that decided who actually suffered and how the story got told afterward, which is close to this tool's own argument.

### Already covered, different angle available

- **California 1861-62:** the tool's Sacramento case, which could be deepened with Jones's framing of it as the largest flood in state history and a template for how the state still underestimates its own flood risk.
- **The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami:** the Simeulue/Aceh material, now further strengthened by the smong research covered elsewhere in this project.
- **Hurricane Katrina:** the creative-arts New Orleans case.
- **Pompeii:** touched in the visual-art document's deep-time section via the plaster casts.
- **Lisbon 1755:** now its own entry in the vernacular-architecture-historical-trends document.
- **2011 Japan:** the tsunami-stones/Aneyoshi material already in the flood-markers document.

### Genuinely new, worth a real look

The 1927 Mississippi flood is probably the strongest candidate in this whole list. It's a purely natural disaster that became one of the twentieth century's clearest civil-rights stories, exactly the kind of case this tool is built to hold: a physical event whose real significance is in what it revealed about the society it hit.

The specific injustice is stark. Black sharecroppers were forcibly conscripted at gunpoint into levee repair work while white residents were evacuated first, a documented, direct hierarchy of whose safety the response actually protected. This wasn't incidental unfairness in the chaos of a disaster response; it was who got ordered to stay and labor versus who got moved to safety, decided along racial lines in the middle of the flood itself.

The grievance didn't stop at the moment of the flood. Historians directly credit it as a factor in the Great Migration north and in shifting Black voters away from the Republican party in the following decade. That combination, an unusually clean, well-documented case where the disaster response itself exposed exactly who a system was built to protect, and where the political consequences of that exposure are traceable for years afterward, makes this the cleanest "Degradation exposes who a system was actually built to protect" case anywhere in this cross-reference.

The 1923 Great Kantō earthquake is also a strong, if darker, candidate. It's covered in more depth in the heritage-destruction-broken-narrative document, since its most significant cultural-memory story for this research is the massacre of Korean residents that followed the earthquake, not the earthquake engineering itself.

Iceland's 1783 Laki eruption is a genuine gap in the tool as it stands. It was a volcanic fissure eruption that killed an estimated 20-25% of Iceland's population through famine and poisoned livestock, a scale of domestic devastation that alone would justify a closer look.

What makes Laki unusual, though, is that its damage didn't stay in Iceland. Its ash cloud is credibly linked to failed harvests and excess deaths as far away as France and Egypt, meaning a single eighteenth-century Icelandic eruption left a documented mortality signature on two other continents. That reach arguably makes Laki one of history's clearest examples of a single disaster's memory operating at a global scale rather than a purely local one, and it's worth a dedicated research pass if it becomes a real candidate; this document is only flagging that the material exists and looks promising, not fully researching it yet.

## A Paradise Built in Hell (Solnit)

The confirmed case list: the 1906 San Francisco earthquake, the 1917 Halifax Explosion, the 1985 Mexico City earthquake, September 11, 2001, and Hurricane Katrina. Solnit's actual argument, worth stating because it cuts against a lot of disaster-response assumptions elsewhere in this research, is that ordinary people's real response to disaster is overwhelmingly mutual aid and spontaneous cooperative community, not panic or violence, and that "elite panic," authorities' fear that the public will turn dangerous, is usually the more destructive force in the room.

### Already covered

- **Mexico City 1985:** already a full case in the tool. Solnit's mutual-aid framing, the "topos" self-organized rescue brigades of ordinary residents digging survivors out with their hands before formal response arrived, would be a strong angle to fold into that case if it isn't already.
- **Hurricane Katrina:** overlaps with the creative-arts case, though again from the mutual-aid angle rather than the art-therapy angle already used there.

### Genuinely new

The 1906 San Francisco earthquake and fire is a clean gap, worth a real look given how much of this research already touches quake-and-rebuild stories: Lisbon, Chicago, Nias, and the pagoda material all sit in the same broader family. Solnit's specific angle on San Francisco is distinct from the engineering focus of those other cases.

Her angle is the refugee camps that sprang up in the city's parks after the quake, which by several accounts functioned so well as self-organized mutual-aid communities that some residents reportedly said afterward they missed them. That's a striking detail on its own: a temporary, leaderless camp of displaced people that some of its own residents later remembered fondly enough to miss once it was gone, which is close to a direct proof of Solnit's broader argument that disaster response defaults to cooperation rather than the panic or predation officials tend to fear.

The Halifax Explosion, a 1917 munitions-ship collision and detonation in Halifax harbor, is worth flagging plainly as technically an industrial and wartime accident rather than a natural hazard, a distinction that would need addressing directly if this becomes a real case for the tool. With that caveat in place, it's still Solnit's earliest example in the book, and by her account, one of her most striking cases of instant, leaderless mutual aid under just about the worst possible conditions. If the tool takes it on despite the non-natural origin, it would sit as the earliest point in a mutual-aid thread running from Halifax through Mexico City's topos brigades to Katrina's New Orleans, three separate disasters, decades apart, all showing the same default human response Solnit is arguing for.

Worth noting plainly: 9/11 sits outside this tool's natural-disaster scope and is probably not a fit regardless of how well Solnit's chapter reads.
