# Statues, sculptures, and plaques built to commemorate disasters

Fifth research pass. Using the Mexico City protest mural and the tool's existing flood-marker cases (Vicksburg, Aneyoshi, Rome) as the reference point, but narrowed here specifically to intentional, built commemorative objects, statues, monuments, plaques, memorial architecture, rather than paintings or festivals covered in the other two documents. Pushed back as far as the record allows.

## Outline

- **The Gilgamesh flood tablet, Mesopotamia**: a 7th-century-BCE cuneiform tablet containing an exact flood-boat construction spec, possibly the oldest surviving object made specifically to carry a disaster-response plan forward.
- **Nilometers, Egypt**: stone stairways and columns in use since the Old Kingdom that measured the Nile's flood height annually, doubling as religious ritual objects tied to the god Hapi.
- **Erwang Temple, Dujiangyan, China**: a temple honoring the engineers of an ancient flood-control system, in continuous use for over two thousand years.
- **The Aneyoshi tsunami stone and counterparts, Japan**: carved warning markers, some centuries old, whose "do not build below this point" instructions were actually obeyed by residents for decades.
- **The 1557 Rome flood plaque and the Vicksburg flood-mark tower**: already documented cases, restated here as belonging to the same genre of physical flood-height markers.
- **Monument to the Great Fire of London**: a 202-foot column completed in 1677 whose height literally encodes its distance from where the 1666 fire started.
- **Halifax Explosion Memorial Bell Tower, Nova Scotia**: a 1985 tower built to house bronze bells donated decades earlier by a woman who lost her family in the 1917 explosion.
- **Johnstown Flood National Memorial, Pennsylvania**: a National Park Service site that preserves the actual failed dam and lakebed responsible for the 1889 flood.
- **The Great Boston Molasses Flood plaque**: a minimal one-sentence marker recording an industrial disaster, kept here as a comparison point for the plaque as a genre.
- **The Mexico City "Monumentos NO reconstrucción" mural and the Whitney's Gabriella Torres-Ferrer piece**: already documented cases showing protest art and repurposed wreckage standing in deliberate opposition to official memorials.

## The full entries

### The Gilgamesh flood tablet, Mesopotamia, story likely centuries old by the time it was inscribed, tablet copied 7th century BCE

The oldest candidate across all three of these documents, and the one you specifically wanted included. Tablet XI of the Epic of Gilgamesh tells how Utnapishtim survives a god-sent flood by building a boat to precise specifications given in the text itself: 120 cubits per side, seven decks, sealed with pitch. That level of detail is unusual for a text that otherwise reads as myth; it is an actual construction spec embedded inside a story about divine punishment and survival.

The tablet was rediscovered in the ruins of Ashurbanipal's library at Nineveh and deciphered by George Smith in 1872, a moment that caused an international sensation for how closely it paralleled the Noah story in Genesis, itself written down roughly a thousand years later. The parallel was close enough that it reshaped how scholars thought about the relationship between Mesopotamian and biblical flood narratives, and Smith's decipherment became one of the defining moments in the early history of Assyriology.

It's not a monument in the built sense, but it may be the oldest surviving physical object anyone made specifically to pass a flood-response plan forward, wrapped in a story so later generations would actually keep telling it. Encoding the instructions inside a memorable narrative rather than a dry technical record may be exactly why it survived: nobody preserves a boat-building manual for three thousand years, but people do keep retelling a story about a god-sent flood and the one man who knew how to survive it. Worth treating as the tool's own founding artifact for the whole idea of formulation.

### Nilometers, Egypt, in use since at least the Old Kingdom, some structures still standing

Purpose-built structures, usually a stone stairway or a column marked in cubits, for measuring the Nile's flood height each year, since the flood's exact level meant the difference between abundance, drought, and destructive overflow. Unlike most of the monuments on this list, a nilometer wasn't built after a specific disaster to commemorate it; it existed to measure the recurring event every single year, disaster and abundance both being possible outcomes of the same flood.

The best-preserved surviving example, the Nilometer on Rhoda Island in Cairo, dates to 861 CE and still stands, a working measurement device that has outlasted the empires that built and later maintained it. Less a memorial than a piece of infrastructure that doubled as a ritual object, its readings were tied directly to religious observance around the god Hapi and, later, the Wafaa El-Nil festival, meaning the same structure that told farmers what to expect from the coming season also anchored a recurring public celebration.

### Erwang Temple, Dujiangyan, Sichuan, China, continuous use since not long after 256 BCE

Built to honor Li Bing and his son for the flood-control irrigation system they engineered, and still the site of an annual water-releasing festival and temple fair over two thousand years later. The irrigation system itself, not just the temple built to honor its creators, has remained in active use for that entire span, making this one of the longest continuously functioning pieces of disaster-mitigation infrastructure anywhere in this research.

It's covered in more depth in the events document, since the annual festival is the more developed thread there, but it belongs here too as a physical monument: a temple complex, not just a festival date, standing as a permanent built object rather than a once-a-year observance that leaves no trace the rest of the year.

### The Aneyoshi tsunami stone and its counterparts along Japan's coast, earliest examples reportedly centuries old, most from the 1896 and 1933 tsunamis

Already deeply covered in the tool, but worth restating in this context: these are stone markers, physically identical in function to a modern plaque, carved with a direct warning, "do not build below this point." That is a rare thing for a monument to do: most of the objects on this list commemorate a disaster after the fact, while these are built explicitly to prevent the next one.

What makes them unusual among monuments on this list is that residents in at least a few villages actually obeyed them for decades, keeping newer construction above the line the stones marked. A monument is normally judged by whether it is remembered; a tsunami stone is judged by whether anyone actually listened, and in at least some villages, they did.

### The 1557 Rome flood plaque and the Vicksburg flood-mark tower, already in the tool

Both are worth naming here specifically as the same genre as everything else on this list: a physical marker built or carved for the explicit purpose of recording a flood's height for people who hadn't lived through it yet. They sit alongside the Aneyoshi stones and the nilometers as evidence that this particular impulse, marking exactly how high the water got, so a later generation can grasp the scale of it in physical terms rather than a written number, recurs across cultures and centuries that had no contact with one another.

### Monument to the Great Fire of London, England, completed 1677

A 202-foot Doric column designed by Christopher Wren and Robert Hooke, standing 202 feet from the spot on Pudding Lane where the 1666 fire is believed to have started, so that its height itself encodes the distance. That design choice turns the monument into a kind of built diagram: the object doesn't just mark where the fire started, its own dimensions are the measurement.

Visitors have been able to climb it since it was built, meaning the monument has functioned as public architecture and a tourist attraction continuously for over three centuries, not as a static thing kept behind glass. It stands today as one of the oldest secular, purpose-built disaster monuments in the Western world that's still standing in its original form and still open to the public, a rare case of an early monument surviving with both its structure and its original public function intact.

### Halifax Explosion Memorial Bell Tower, Fort Needham Memorial Park, Nova Scotia, Canada, bells first cast soon after 1917, tower built 1985

Ten of the fourteen bronze bells housed in the tower were originally donated to a nearby church by Barbara Orr, a survivor who lost her parents and four siblings in the 1917 explosion. That initial donation was a private act of grief and remembrance decades before any public monument existed, a single person choosing to memorialize her own family through bells rather than through a plaque bearing their names.

The standalone tower was built decades later, in 1985, specifically to hold those bells, converting a private family donation into a permanent public monument. It's a rare case of a single grieving family's private donation becoming a city's permanent public monument, with the physical objects at the center of the memorial predating the memorial's own architecture by close to seventy years.

### Johnstown Flood National Memorial, Pennsylvania, USA, established 1964

A US National Park Service site preserving the remains of the South Fork Dam and part of the old Lake Conemaugh bed, the actual physical cause of the 1889 flood that killed over 2,200 people, rather than a monument built somewhere symbolic. Unlike most entries on this list, there was no design decision about where to place a commemorative object; the site simply preserves the place where the failure happened.

The failure site itself is the memorial, which makes this case unusual in the company of stone markers and cast bells: there is no artist, architect, or sculptor mediating between the disaster and the visitor, only the remains of the dam that broke.

### The Great Boston Molasses Flood plaque, Boston, Massachusetts, USA, placed later 20th century

A small marker in the North End reading simply: "On January 15, 1919, a molasses tank at 529 Commercial Street, exploded under pressure, killing 21 people." Flagging this one the same way Bhopal and Chernobyl got flagged in the culture document: it's an industrial accident, not a natural hazard, sitting slightly outside the usual scope of this research.

Even so, it's such a clean, minimal example of the plaque-as-genre, one sentence, one date, one number, that it's worth having on hand as a formal comparison point against the far more elaborate monuments elsewhere on this list, a reminder that a monument doesn't need scale or artistry to do its basic job of stating what happened and how many died.

### The "Monumentos NO reconstrucción" mural, Mexico City, 2017, and the Whitney's Gabriella Torres-Ferrer piece, 2018, both already in the tool

Named here as your own reference point, and worth noting they sit at the opposite end of this list from the ancient examples. Instead of a state or institution building a monument, these are residents and artists actively protesting an official memorial as a substitute for real material support, or repurposing wreckage itself, an actual fallen utility post, into the monument.

That inversion matters against everything else in this document: the Gilgamesh tablet, the nilometers, the Erwang Temple, and the London and Halifax monuments were all built by, or with the blessing of, authority, whether a king, a temple bureaucracy, or a city government. These two pieces exist because artists and residents didn't trust the official version of commemoration to mean anything without material follow-through, making them the clearest example in this whole set of a monument functioning as protest rather than as sanctioned memory.
