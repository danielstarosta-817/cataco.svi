# Greece, Turkey, and Spain: the early-2020s Mediterranean wildfire seasons

Three countries, the same few summers (mainly 2021 for Greece and Turkey, 2022 for Spain, all part of the same run of record-breaking Mediterranean fire seasons), and a different flavor of the same theme running through the rest of this research: fire hitting landscapes that are themselves the physical evidence of a very long cultural memory, so the loss reads as cultural before anyone even gets to the practical damage.

## Outline

- **North Evia, Greece (August 2021):** An eight-day fire that consumed roughly 210,000 acres of resin-and-honey pine forest and ancient olive groves, described by Greek commentators as an assault on "living archives" of cultural continuity, not just an economic loss.
- **Antalya and Muğla provinces, Turkey (July–August 2021):** Turkey's worst fire season in decades, in which the village of Ahmetler organized and funded its own firefighting defense after state support failed to arrive in time, and came through with no structures or lives lost.
- **Spain (summer 2022):** The country's second-worst wildfire year on record, in which fires threatened UNESCO World Heritage sites like Atapuerca and Medina Azahara and exposed how rural depopulation left previously tended land newly vulnerable to fire.
- **The shared thread across all three:** Unlike the suppression-driven fire stories elsewhere in this research, these cases show landscapes that are themselves cultural artifacts being destroyed by fire regimes worsening on the same accelerating timeline as the hurricane material.

## The full writeups

**North Evia, Greece (August 2021).** The fire burned for eight days, from August 3 to 11, and consumed roughly 210,000 acres in total, including about 155,000 acres of forest. It is counted among the worst ecological disasters in Greece's modern history, and the specific character of what burned matters as much as the scale. This was not generic woodland; it was pine forest that supplies a majority of Greece's resin and conifer honey production, so the fire wiped out a working livelihood at the same time it destroyed the forest itself.

Layered on top of that economic loss is a cultural one. The burned area also included olive groves and pine stands that Greek commentators explicitly describe as "living archives," carrying the memory of generations in the literal landscape. The olive tree in particular has been a central emblem of Greek identity since antiquity, so its loss to fire reads differently than the loss of an ordinary crop; it is closer to losing a physical link to the ancient world than to losing a season's yield.

A regional piece from Osservatorio Balcani Caucaso Transeuropa frames the recurring fires in exactly these terms, arguing that they produce genuine collective trauma rather than simple property loss, precisely because the burned landscape is also the physical record of continuity with antiquity. That framing, an ecological disaster read simultaneously as an act of cultural erasure, is the throughline connecting all three cases in this document.

**Antalya and Muğla provinces, Turkey (July-August 2021).** This was Turkey's worst fire season in decades: roughly 150,000 hectares burned along the southwest Mediterranean coast, eight deaths were confirmed, and 180 separate fires broke out across 38 provinces. Against that scale of loss, the strongest concrete story is a single village, Ahmetler, in Antalya's Manavgat district, where state firefighting support did not arrive in time.

Rather than wait, Ahmetler's residents pooled their own money to hire private fire trucks and organized their own defense of the village. The result was as close to a clean success as this research finds anywhere in the Mediterranean fire seasons: the village came through with no structures lost and no human or animal deaths. Research comparing centralized versus local fire response points to Ahmetler specifically as evidence that local terrain knowledge outperformed the centralized forest-management paradigm that failed to reach the village in time.

Months later, a livestock breeder from the region gave a plain, on-record comment that is worth keeping for what it captures in a single sentence: "the air is not the same" anymore. It is a first-person statement of the same climate-shifted-baseline idea that runs through the hurricane-party material elsewhere in this research, someone living through the change noticing, in the most ordinary possible language, that the world around them has already shifted.

**Spain (summer 2022).** Spain's 2022 fire season was its second-worst on record after 2012, with more than 300,000 hectares burned nationally. The Sierra de la Culebra fire in Zamora alone is counted among the largest single fires ever recorded in the country, a scale that puts the season in context before even getting to what, specifically, was at risk.

The cultural-heritage angle is the sharpest part of this case. Spanish commentary on the fires points out that several of the country's UNESCO World Heritage sites, including the prehistoric Atapuerca archaeological complex and the Caliphate city of Medina Azahara, sit inside fire-vulnerable green zones. Wildfire risk in Spain is explicitly framed, in this commentary, as a threat to physical, ancient-through-medieval historical memory, not merely to modern property or timber.

A related thread is worth naming on its own: several Spanish and pan-European recovery projects argue that the fire risk itself is partly a rural-abandonment problem. Land that used to be actively grazed, farmed, or otherwise tended, much like the controlled-burn material already covered in the fire-and-ritual document, became fire-vulnerable specifically once the people and practices that used to manage it left. It is an inversion of the pattern found elsewhere where ritual and practical tool were the same act: here, the ritual and the practice stopped, and the fire risk grew back into the gap they left behind.

**The shared thread across all three.** Most of the fire material already in this research, Australia, California, Brazil, has a throughline of deliberate suppression of a traditional practice. These three Mediterranean cases work differently: all three are about landscapes that are themselves cultural artifacts, ancient olive groves, resin pine forests tied to conifer honey, UNESCO-listed archaeological sites, being destroyed by fire regimes that have measurably worsened in the same twenty-year window as the rapid-intensification hurricane material covered elsewhere.

If this research wants a clean argument that climate change is compressing the timeline from formulation to degradation, pairing this document with the hurricane-parties one is probably the most direct way to make it. Two different hazards point to the same underlying claim: the pace of change now regularly outruns the cultural practices and landscapes that took centuries to form.
