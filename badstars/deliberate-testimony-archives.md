# Deliberate testimony archives: communities building their own Yad Vashem

The right way to organize this: not disaster memory that survives by accident (oral tradition, songs, murals), but a community or institution making a conscious decision to systematically collect elders' and survivors' first-person accounts before they're lost, and build a real, structured repository, an archive, a trained storyteller corps, a published book, specifically so the next generation inherits testimony rather than just summary. Six real examples, spanning very different scales and methods.

## Outline

- **Kataribe, Kobe, Japan:** more than 150 survivors of the 1995 Hanshin-Awaji earthquake volunteer as trained storytellers, walking visitors and schoolchildren through the city with firsthand accounts rather than a curated exhibit's version of events.
- **The 3.11 Densho Road network and Tohoku's kataribe, Japan:** over 300 registered memorial sites across Tohoku are now connected into a single network built after the 2011 tsunami, offering guided tours and survivor-led storytelling at preserved sites like Nakahama Elementary School.
- **Voices from the Dust Bowl, Library of Congress:** folklorists recorded roughly 18 hours of migrant testimony on a 50-pound disc recorder in 1940 and 1941, an archive still publicly available today, 85 years later.
- **Voices from the Storm, New Orleans:** a 2008 book collecting thirteen New Orleanians' first-person oral histories of Hurricane Katrina, later adapted into a stage script by Oakland high school students.
- **UC QuakeStudies / CEISMIC, Christchurch, New Zealand:** a university-run digital archive launched in 2012 holding more than 55,000 items on the Canterbury earthquakes, including over 700 recorded testimonies in 13 languages.
- **The Aceh Tsunami Museum's Survivor Stories collection, Banda Aceh, Indonesia:** a museum that doubles as an emergency tsunami shelter also houses a dedicated video and written testimony collection placed alongside its scientific exhibits.

## Kataribe, Kobe, Japan, ongoing since shortly after the 1995 Hanshin-Awaji earthquake

The closest single match to the Yad Vashem model in spirit: formal, sustained, and built around trained witnesses rather than passive archives. Kataribe (literally "storytellers," a term with deep roots in Japanese oral tradition generally) are earthquake survivors, more than 150 of them at the Great Hanshin-Awaji Earthquake Memorial alone, who volunteer to walk visitors and especially schoolchildren through the city and the memorial, sharing their own direct experience of the quake rather than a curated exhibit's version of it.

Their accounts are specifically valued for carrying "emotion, hesitation, and decision-making under pressure" that a static exhibit can't reproduce. A survivor pausing mid-sentence, or describing the specific choice they made in a moment of panic, transmits something a printed placard cannot.

The program has continued for three decades, meaning some of today's kataribe were children themselves in 1995, now old enough to be the ones testifying. That generational handoff, the child who lived through the quake growing up to become the person telling the story to the next generation of schoolchildren, is itself a kind of proof that the model works as designed.

## The 3.11 Densho Road network and Tohoku's kataribe, Japan, built out over the decade following the 2011 tsunami

A direct continuation of Kobe's model at a larger, more infrastructural scale: over 300 registered memorial sites across the Tōhoku region are now connected into a single network, each offering some combination of guided tours, workshops, and survivor-led storytelling, rather than existing as isolated, disconnected memorials.

At preserved sites like Nakahama Elementary School, the guide is often a named survivor with direct authority over the story. In that specific case, the school's former vice principal at the time of the tsunami walks visitors through the building where students and staff sheltered above the water, a firsthand account from someone who was actually responsible for the children in the room that day.

The explicit stated goal across the network is preventing complacency in future generations by keeping firsthand testimony, not just information, in circulation. Scaling Kobe's single-city model to an entire region suggests the kataribe approach was treated as something worth replicating deliberately, not a one-off local tradition.

## Voices from the Dust Bowl, Library of Congress, recorded 1940 to 1941, still archived and public today

The oldest and most purely archival example here, worth including specifically because it shows the same impulse predates any of the modern institutional models. Folklorists Charles Todd and Robert Sonkin carried a 50-pound disc recorder into Farm Security Administration migrant camps across central California and recorded roughly 18 hours of migrants from Oklahoma, Texas, Arkansas, and Missouri, in their own words, while the Dust Bowl displacement was still actively unfolding, not reconstructed decades later from memory.

The full collection is still publicly available today through the Library of Congress's digital collections, an 85-year-old repository that functions exactly like a modern oral-history archive, just built with 1940s equipment. That durability across nearly a century, recordings made on heavy analog disc equipment and now streamable online, shows how far a well-kept archive can outlast the technology used to build it.

Notably, this collection was assembled by outside folklorists rather than the affected community itself, worth flagging as a different authorship model than the community-led examples elsewhere in this document. Todd and Sonkin were documentarians visiting the camps, not migrants recording their own neighbors, a distinction that matters when comparing who controls the testimony versus who simply appears in it.

## Voices from the Storm, New Orleans, published 2008, part of the Voice of Witness book series

A community-testimony project in book form rather than a physical archive or a trained-guide network: journalist Lola Vollen and McSweeney's editor Chris Ying collected and edited thirteen New Orleanians' full first-person oral histories of Hurricane Katrina and its aftermath into a single published volume, explicitly centering race and class in how the disaster was actually lived through, rather than how it was covered.

That editorial choice matters. Where much of the initial media coverage of Katrina focused on flood mechanics, government failure, and aggregate statistics, the book stays with individual, named people describing their own experience of the storm and its aftermath in full.

The book has since taken on a second life as an actual teaching tool: high school theater students at Oakland Technical High School adapted the thirteen oral histories directly into a stage script performed for the public, testimony being deliberately re-transmitted a second time, through a different medium, to a new audience that wasn't there for the original disaster.

## UC QuakeStudies / CEISMIC, Christchurch, New Zealand, launched 2012, still growing

The largest-scale, most purely digital example, and the clearest case of a university taking on the Yad Vashem-style institutional-archive role directly. The University of Canterbury's CEISMIC archive now holds more than 55,000 publicly accessible items related to the 2010 to 2011 Canterbury earthquakes, and its QuakeBox sub-project alone recorded over 700 members of the public describing their own experiences, in 13 different languages, from booths set up at nine locations around the city.

That multilingual, multi-site design meant the archive captured testimony from residents who might otherwise have been left out of a purely English-language, single-location project, a deliberate attempt to make the archive represent the whole city rather than just its most easily reached residents.

A parallel, deliberately narrower project, Women's Voices, run by the Christchurch branch of the National Council of Women, trained more than 30 volunteer interviewers specifically to collect 100 interviews with women in the hardest-hit suburbs. It stands as a smaller, community-organized archive nested inside the larger university one, worth noting as a model for how a general archive and a targeted, community-specific one can coexist and reinforce each other.

## The Aceh Tsunami Museum's Survivor Stories collection, Banda Aceh, Indonesia, museum opened 2009

Worth including specifically because it deepens the tool's own existing Aceh case rather than standing apart from it. The museum was built to function as an actual emergency tsunami shelter as well as an exhibition space, a piece of hazard infrastructure and a memory institution combined into one building.

Alongside that shelter function, the museum built a dedicated Survivor Stories section combining video and written testimony, intentionally placed next to scientific exhibits explaining the physical mechanics of the wave. A visitor encounters the human account and the hazard science side by side rather than one substituting for the other, pairing the emotional record of what happened with the technical explanation of why it happened at the same stop on the tour.

## What ties all six together, and what's genuinely different about them

Every example here required someone to make an active, resourced decision, hire folklorists, train volunteers, fund a museum wing, commission a book, build a university archive, rather than simply letting testimony circulate informally the way smong or the Thunderbird-and-Whale tradition did.

The real variation worth noting for the tool is who does the collecting: outside researchers in the Dust Bowl case, survivors themselves in the kataribe case, a mixed journalist-and-subject model in Katrina, volunteers trained by an institution in Christchurch. What form the result takes, a walking tour, a book, a searchable digital archive, a museum wing, turns out to matter just as much as the underlying impulse, which is identical across all six: catch the testimony before the generation that lived it is gone.
