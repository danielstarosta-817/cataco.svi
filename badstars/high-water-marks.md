# High-water marks, as their own category

A deeper dive on one specific thing already in the monuments document: the plaque or marker that exists purely to record how high a flood actually got, for people who weren't there. This uses the tool's own Vicksburg, Aneyoshi, and Rome markers as the throughline, pulled out here as their own focused list, plus a few real variations on the same idea. I couldn't confirm a specific historic marker on the Ganges despite a few different searches, noted honestly below rather than guessed at, but the tradition clearly exists in other river systems in a way that's well documented.

## In this document

- **Vicksburg, Mississippi; Aneyoshi, Japan; Rome, Italy:** The reference set already covered in the tool, three different centuries and materials doing the same basic job of recording a flood's height.
- **Passau, Germany:** The Rathaus wall carries a running chart of flood heights back to the 11th century, and a 2013 flood finally topped a mark set in 1501.
- **The Zouave of Pont de l'Alma, Paris, France:** A decorative 1856 statue became Paris's unofficial flood gauge after the catastrophic 1910 flood submerged it to the shoulders.
- **Baiheliang (White Crane Ridge), Chongqing, China:** A submerged rock ridge carved since 763 CE tracks the river's lowest safe water levels rather than its highest, a drought gauge instead of a flood one.
- **The Ganges:** An honest gap. The tradition of flood-marking almost certainly exists on the river, but I could not locate a specific, named historic marker to cite.

## Vicksburg, Aneyoshi, and Rome

These three are the reference set for this whole document, already covered elsewhere in the tool: a modern gage tower carrying a century of flood lines in Vicksburg, Mississippi; a Meiji-era tsunami warning stone in Aneyoshi, Japan, explicitly telling people not to build below it; and a 16th-century marble plaque on a Roman church recording a flood's height for a congregation that hadn't lived through it.

What makes them worth naming again here, rather than just leaving them in the monuments document, is that together they establish the basic shape of the category this document is tracking: three different centuries, three different materials, wood, stone, marble, and the same basic act underneath all of them, a fixed physical mark left specifically so a later reader could know exactly how high the water got without having to take anyone's word for it.

## Passau, Germany

Passau is probably the single richest version of this idea anywhere. The city sits at the meeting point of three rivers, the Danube, Inn, and Ilz, and has flooded repeatedly for as long as it has had written records, with the earliest documented high water in the city recorded from the year 1060.

The wall of the Rathaus, Passau's city hall, carries a running chart of flood heights that reads almost like a timeline of the city's worst days: 1501, 1595, 1787, 1862, 1899, 1920, and 1954 are all marked directly on the building. The 1501 mark is set high enough that it sits above the building's own entrance archway, a detail that makes the scale of that particular flood immediately legible to anyone walking up to the door centuries later without needing to read a single word of explanation.

That five-century-old high mark held its record for a very long time, until 2013, when a new flood finally topped even the 1501 line, the highest water level recorded in the city in over 500 years. The Rathaus wall is, in effect, a live document: it had been accurate for five centuries, and then it had to be updated.

## The Zouave of Pont de l'Alma, Paris

Unlike every other entry in this document, the Zouave is not a plaque at all, and it wasn't built to measure anything. It's a decorative stone statue of a soldier, one of four originally installed in 1856 to flank a bridge over the Seine, a purely ornamental addition to the cityscape with no flood function built into its design.

That changed by accident. The catastrophic Paris flood of 1910 submerged the statue up to its shoulders, and from that point on, Parisians began using the Zouave informally as the city's flood gauge, judging a flood's severity by how much of the statue's body had disappeared under the water. It became a fixed, familiar, and thoroughly unofficial answer to the same question every deliberately built marker in this document was designed to answer.

The tradition has persisted, and Parisians reportedly still gauge flood severity by the Zouave today. It comes with one real caveat worth keeping honest: the bridge, and the statue's exact placement on it, was rebuilt in the 1970s, which technically makes the modern comparison a bit shakier than it used to be, since the statue people check today isn't standing in precisely the same spot as the one that went under in 1910.

## Baiheliang (White Crane Ridge), Chongqing, China

Baiheliang is the inverse of every other entry on this list. Instead of marking flood danger, this submerged rock ridge in the Yangtze River was carved over twelve centuries, starting in 763 CE during the Tang dynasty, with fish figures whose eyes marked the river's lowest safe water levels rather than its highest. It is a drought gauge, not a flood one, and it is worth including specifically because it reframes the whole category: the water level that matters to a community isn't always the high one.

The scale of the record is remarkable in its own right. The carvings track 72 low-water years across more than a thousand years of continuous documentation, a running record of drought severity kept by a community that needed to know exactly how bad a dry year was getting, in the same way Passau's residents needed to know exactly how bad a flood year was getting.

The ridge is now preserved inside a dedicated underwater museum, built after the Three Gorges Dam permanently raised the river's level and submerged the site for good. Visitors view the thousand-year-old carvings today through viewing tunnels and glass, a modern engineering solution built specifically to keep a drought record legible even after the same modern engineering that necessitated it made the ridge impossible to see with the naked eye.

## On the Ganges specifically

You mentioned very high Ganges flood markers as something you recalled existing, and it's entirely plausible. Varanasi's ghats flood on a regular, well-documented basis, and Indian river cities in general have both formal modern gauge stations, the Central Water Commission's "danger mark" system, and informal painted or chalked flood lines that get redrawn after major events.

I wasn't able to turn up a specific named, historic, physically inscribed marker the way Passau or Rome have, in the time I spent searching. This is worth a more targeted pass later, possibly starting from a specific city, Varanasi, Patna, or Allahabad/Prayagraj, and a specific known flood year, rather than approaching the river as a whole.
