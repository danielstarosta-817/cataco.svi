# Children: teaching them what happened, and helping them process it

A deliberate expansion of the tool's existing children's-arts-therapy material (Flying Seagull, Za'atari, the Peregian and creative-arts New Orleans cases), reaching past the "handing a kid a crayon" register into two other things adults and institutions have built for children specifically: disaster education meant to actually change behavior before the next one, and formal, clinical-adjacent trauma care after it. Organized in that order, since the strongest case here, Kamaishi, sits right at the seam between the two.

## Outline

- **Tsunami tendenko and the "Miracle of Kamaishi," Japan:** a tsunami-evacuation curriculum built for schoolchildren from 2005 led to a 99.8% survival rate among the city's students when the actual tsunami hit in March 2011.
- **Child Friendly Spaces, Save the Children and the wider humanitarian sector:** a standardized, low-cost model deployed globally that combines supervised play, informal schooling, and trained adult support to restore a sense of normalcy for displaced children.
- **Ahlan Simsim, Sesame Workshop and the International Rescue Committee:** the largest early-childhood humanitarian intervention in history, using Arabic-language Muppets modeling displacement to reach over 1.3 million children across the Middle East.
- **Kokoro no kea, Japan:** a school-embedded psychosocial care framework built after the 1995 Kobe earthquake to translate Western PTSD treatment into something Japanese teachers could run in their own classrooms.
- **Chernobyl Children International's Rest and Recuperation Programme, Ireland:** since 1991, over 25,500 children from contaminated regions of Belarus have been brought to Ireland for month-long respite stays that measurably cut their body's radiation levels.

## Teaching what happened, and what to do about it

**Tsunami tendenko and the "Miracle of Kamaishi," Japan, training built from 2005, tested March 11, 2011.** Possibly the single best-evidenced children's disaster-education program in existence, and it belongs directly alongside the smong material already in the oral-tradition document, a different technology aimed at the exact same problem. Starting in 2005 at the request of Kamaishi's own city education council, engineering professor Toshitaka Katada built a curriculum for local schoolchildren teaching tsunami tendenko, roughly "every man for himself" in a tsunami, alongside two blunt rules: don't trust your assumptions about how bad a disaster will be, and evacuate immediately without waiting for instructions.

Students studied local geology and the region's tsunami history, watched footage from the 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami, and ran real evacuation drills, with older students specifically trained to shepherd younger ones to high ground rather than waiting for adult direction. The curriculum treated the drill itself as the actual content, not a supplement to a classroom lesson, on the theory that a child's body needs to already know the evacuation route before a real disaster arrives.

When the actual tsunami hit in March 2011, Kamaishi's schoolchildren evacuated on their own initiative, without waiting for teachers, exactly as drilled. Of roughly 2,900 primary and secondary students in the city, only five died, and all five were away from school that day: a 99.8% survival rate, credited directly to the education program rather than luck, in a city where the tsunami itself reached the schools minutes after the children had already left them.

**Child Friendly Spaces, Save the Children and the wider humanitarian sector, standardized model, deployed globally.** The institutional backbone underneath most disaster-zone children's programs, worth naming as its own thing rather than folding into any single case. A Child Friendly Space can be built almost anywhere fast and cheap: a tent, a courtyard, a spot under a tree. It combines three functions at once, supervised play, informal continued schooling, and a structured, trained-adult presence for children to talk to about what they've just been through.

That third function does double duty, since it also frees parents to handle the logistics of survival, food, shelter, paperwork, without having to manage their children's daily needs at the same time. The arrangement treats a child's stability and an adult's capacity to cope as linked problems rather than separate ones.

The explicit design goal is restoring a sense of normalcy and routine as fast as possible after displacement, which functions as a kind of trauma triage. It is a stopgap rather than deep clinical treatment, one that keeps ordinary childhood running enough that formal therapy, if it comes later, has less ground to make up.

## Formal and media-based trauma care

**Ahlan Simsim, Sesame Workshop and the International Rescue Committee, launched 2020, ongoing.** The largest early-childhood intervention in the history of humanitarian response, built specifically around trauma and resilience rather than general education. Two new Arabic-language Muppets, Jad (a boy forced to leave his home) and Basma (the girl who welcomes him), model exactly the displacement experience the child audience is living through, aimed at children in Syria, Iraq, Jordan, and Lebanon and reaching over 1.3 million children and caregivers with direct services.

The program has since proven flexible enough to redeploy fast: content built for conflict displacement was adapted and pushed out again after the 2020 Beirut port explosion, then again for children affected by the Morocco and Turkey/Syria earthquakes and the Libya floods. That reuse treats a Muppet show as reusable trauma-response infrastructure rather than a one-off production, something that can be repointed at a new disaster within weeks rather than built from scratch each time.

Independent research from NYU's Global TIES for Children has found measurable gains in the program's target children's language, numeracy, and social-emotional development, a rare case of a creative-arts intervention with an actual outcomes study behind it rather than just anecdotal testimony about its value.

**Kokoro no kea ("care for the heart/mind"), Japan, built after the 1995 Kobe earthquake, still in use.** Japan's own home-grown, school-embedded psychosocial care system, developed specifically because the Western PTSD diagnostic framework arrived in Japan alongside the Kobe disaster and had to be translated into something schools could actually run. Roughly 8,800 schoolchildren across three grade levels in the disaster zone were formally studied for psychological symptoms after the quake.

The resulting framework built "children's mental care in schools" directly into the education system, with teachers trained to watch for and respond to trauma symptoms in their own classrooms, rather than routing every affected child out to a separate clinical system. This kept care embedded in a space children already occupied every day instead of requiring a second, unfamiliar institution.

The same infrastructure was activated again after the 2011 Tōhoku disaster, with teachers explicitly tasked with combining traumatic-stress care and disaster-risk-reduction education in the same lessons, the clearest institutional link in this whole document between teaching what happened and helping children process it, treated as one job rather than two.

**Chernobyl Children International's Rest and Recuperation Programme, Ireland, founded 1991, ongoing.** Distinct from everything else here because it's neither education nor talk therapy: physical removal is the actual mechanism of care. Founded by Adi Roche after an appeal from Belarusian doctors, the program has brought over 25,500 children from Chernobyl-contaminated regions of Belarus to Ireland for month-long respite stays.

During those stays, documented radiation levels in the children's bodies drop by nearly half, adding an estimated two years to each child's life expectancy on average. That is a concrete, measurable physiological effect from removal alone, distinct from anything psychological or educational that the other programs in this document offer.

The program is worth including specifically because it reframes what "processing" a disaster can mean for a child. It is a recurring, decades-long act of literal removal from an ongoing hazard that, unlike almost everything else in this research, never actually ends: plutonium's 24,400-year half-life means these children's own children may eventually need the same program.

## Where this leaves the existing arts-therapy material

Read against these five, the tool's existing Flying Seagull/Za'atari/Peregian/creative-arts cluster sits closer to the Child Friendly Space end of the spectrum: informal, play-based, adult-facilitated but not clinical. Kamaishi and kokoro no kea sit at the more structured, institutionally embedded end, while Ahlan Simsim and the Chernobyl program each represent a genuinely different delivery mechanism, mass media and physical relocation, that doesn't fit neatly on that spectrum at all.

Worth treating this as roughly a four-part taxonomy for the tool if it wants one: informal creative outlet, structured school-based curriculum, mass-media/parasocial intervention, and physical removal from ongoing harm, four different answers to the same underlying question of what a child actually needs after living through a disaster.
