# How We Got Here: Draft Chapters

*Working draft. Chronological spine, thematic depth nested inside each checkpoint. Not yet formatted for the tool; content first, structure later.*

## 1. 1967: The Ground Remembers

On July 29, 1967, a magnitude 6.5–6.6 earthquake hit the Venezuelan coast about 20 kilometers from Caracas. Four apartment towers in Altamira and Los Palos Grandes (Edificio San José, Mijagual, Neverí, and Palace/Corvin) collapsed completely. Mansión Charaima and the Hotel Macuto Sheraton in Caraballeda and Macuto lost most of their floors. Two hundred to 236 people died, about 2,000 were injured, 40 buildings were declared uninhabitable, 180 more were severely damaged.

This is not a story about corruption. Not yet, at least. In 1967, Venezuela was a functioning democracy, oil-wealthy, thirty-one years from Chávez, with none of the "notorious," weaponized cronyism that defines the later chapters of this story. The ground did this anyway. That's the point of starting here: the seismic risk under Caracas and the Vargas coast isn't a symptom of anything that came later. It's older than all of it, and it was never going away. What changes across the next fifty-nine years isn't the risk. It's the country's capacity to answer it.

---

## 2. The Boom Years: Wealth Without a Floor

By 1950, Venezuela had the fourth-highest GDP per capita on Earth, twice as rich as Chile, four times richer than Japan, twelve times richer than China. It stayed the richest major economy in Latin America into the 1980s. The 1970s OPEC oil shock quadrupled its revenues; between 1974 and 1979 the government spent more, in absolute terms, than in the entirety of its prior independent history.

None of that wealth ever had to prove it could survive contact with a real crisis. A country that rich, on a single commodity, doesn't need deep institutions: it needs a functioning oil ministry and enough cash to paper over everything else. Nobody found out whether Venezuela's civil-protection agencies, its seismic monitoring network, its building-code enforcement could hold up under pressure, because the money always arrived before anyone had to ask.

Some of the oil money built real things: schools, hospitals, roads, agricultural subsidies. But a lot of it went somewhere else. Under President Jaime Lusinchi in the mid-1980s, a currency-exchange program called RECADI was supposed to hand out cheap, government-subsidized dollars to importers. Instead, well-connected buyers purchased those dollars at the artificial official rate and resold them on the real market for enormous profit; the Cato Institute later estimated $36 billion moved through the scheme. Public-sector employment doubled under Pérez. State-owned enterprises lost money by design. And despite a genuine oil windfall, the government was still borrowing so heavily to cover 1970s-era nationalizations and social spending that Venezuela carried the fourth-highest debt burden in Latin America. The exact mechanism at the center of RECADI (buy dollars cheap at an official rate, sell high on the street) would resurface almost word-for-word two decades later under a completely different government (see 2005). This wasn't a one-time mistake so much as a design flaw the country would keep rebuilding.

The people who benefited from schemes like RECADI weren't a single, easily labeled group. Some of the same people who spent later decades as opposition figures, including some who only got vocal once they were safely abroad, made real money off currency arbitrage like this themselves. That isn't necessarily hypocrisy on its own. For most of this era, proximity to state contracts and preferential exchange rates was one of the only reliable paths to a stable living in Venezuela, and plenty of people ended up inside that system by necessity rather than conviction. The same arrangement that enriched insiders also left almost anyone who wanted an ordinary life with few ways to build one that didn't run through it somehow.

Then, in the mid-1980s, a global oil glut cut prices by roughly 70% in under a year. An economy that had never diversified had nothing left to fall back on.

## 3. 1989–1992: The Cracks Show

On February 27, 1989, President Carlos Andrés Pérez announced an austerity package meant to address the debt the boom years had left behind, among the changes a 30% overnight hike in public transport fares. Riots and looting broke out in Guarenas and spread to Caracas within a day. The government declared a curfew and activated Plan Ávila, putting the capital under martial law and soldiers in the streets with live ammunition. Most of the dead were shot from the waist up, at night, during curfew: residents of 23 de Enero, El Valle, Petare, and Catia described soldiers firing indiscriminately into apartment buildings in response to a handful of snipers. The official death toll was 276; other estimates run as high as 3,000, never properly counted amid a censored press and a government that never conducted a real investigation. Venezuelans call it the Caracazo.

Three years later, on February 4, 1992, army units led by a mid-ranking officer named Hugo Chávez tried to seize Miraflores Palace, the defense ministry, La Carlota air base, and the Military Museum in a coup attempt against Pérez. It failed, and Chávez went to prison, but the attempt made him a national figure anyway. On November 27, 1992, a second coup attempt followed, this one led by air force and navy officers coordinating with Chávez from his cell, more carefully planned than the first. It was also put down, at a far higher cost: 172 dead. Most of the surviving plotters fled to Peru, granted asylum by Alberto Fujimori.

By 1998, when Chávez ran for president as a civilian, he wasn't an outsider promising something new out of nowhere. He was the face of a rebellion against a government that had already shot its own citizens in the street over a bus fare, and had only barely survived two attempts to overthrow it by force. The oil boom's collapse hadn't just produced abstract debt statistics: it produced the Caracazo's bodies and 1992's coups, and Chávez was elected directly out of that specific wreckage.

## 4. 1998: Hugo Chávez

Hugo Chávez was elected president in December 1998. This chapter is deliberately short, because 1998 itself isn't the catastrophe: it's the pivot after which everything else in this story happens under one consolidating political project, for better and for worse. What that project becomes, and what it does to the country's ability to survive its own geography, is the rest of the story.

---

## 5. 1999: Vargas

Fourteen months after Chávez took office, on December 14–16, 1999, extreme rainfall (up to 911mm over a few days) triggered debris flows along roughly 100 kilometers of the Vargas coast, burying entire neighborhoods between Catia La Mar and past Caruao. It wasn't the country's first warning: the 1967 earthquake, thirty-two years earlier and on the same stretch of coast, had already shown what weak construction and thin state capacity meant for this land. The response gave a preview of habits that would recur for decades: the government's attention that week was on a referendum for Chávez's new constitution, scheduled for December 15, the same day the rain turned into landslides, and no evacuation was organized before the worst of it hit. In the weeks after, two US Navy ships offering personnel and equipment, the USS Nashville and USS Tortuga, were initially turned away, even as Chávez separately accepted a US Army emergency team on the ground, and security forces sent in to stop looting were accused of abuses of their own.

The death toll is disputed: official counts land near 700–3,000; academic estimates run as high as 10,000–30,000, a gap largely blamed on how poorly informal settlements were ever counted in the first place. A population-based reconstruction by anthropologist Rogelio Altez, comparing Vargas's pre-disaster population of roughly 308,000 to the roughly 230,000 counted in the December 2000 census against forensic records of 521 confirmed dead and 331 missing, argues the real toll sits far closer to the low end: the three hardest-hit parishes, Caraballeda, Macuto, and Naiguatá, had a combined population of only about 68,000 before the disaster, too few people to support the 15,000-to-50,000 estimates that still circulate. In a separate river basin in Barlovento, Miranda, the El Guamito dam collapsed the same week, wiping out the town of El Guapo, a second, distinct disaster that gets far less attention than the coastal one.

This isn't a story about the rain being anyone's fault. It's the first real stress test, in a generation, of whatever disaster-response capacity Venezuela still had, and it revealed how thin that capacity already was, one year into a new era that hadn't built anything yet, for better or worse.

## 6. 2005: The Second Time

In February 2005, it happened again. The Pacairigua river and Silma stream overflowed in Guatire; the Guaire river overflowed through Caracas during a record 84.7mm rainfall in 24 hours; Barlovento flooded again. Seven states (Falcón, Yaracuy, Carabobo, Aragua, Miranda, Vargas, Distrito Capital) were placed under national emergency. 178,623 people affected, 24,398 displaced, 62 confirmed dead, 37,021 homes damaged nationally.

For the first time in this story, a disaster repeats inside living memory: people who survived 1999 watching it happen again. But the more important thing happening in 2005 is under the water, not in it: the currency-control system created in 2003 (CADIVI) is now two years old, and the arbitrage economy between the official exchange rate and the black market is becoming a way of life for anyone positioned to profit from the gap. This is the moment the economic bottom starts becoming visible at the same time the ground proves, a second time, that it hasn't forgotten 1999.

## 7. 2017: The Mother of All Marches

On March 29, 2017, Venezuela's Supreme Court, packed with Maduro loyalists, stripped the opposition-controlled National Assembly of its legislative powers and effectively assumed them itself. The National Assembly was the one branch of government the opposition actually held, having won it in a landslide in 2015. The court's move was, functionally, a self-coup: the government dissolving the last national body it didn't control, through a legal ruling instead of a tank.

The response came three weeks later, and the government didn't wait for it quietly. Two days before the march, Maduro ordered the Venezuelan National Militia expanded to 500,000 members, each one promised a rifle, framing it as insurance against a repeat of the 2002 coup attempt against him. Diosdado Cabello, already a senior PSUV official, said 60,000 motorized colectivos and the militia would be spread across Caracas on April 19, "until necessary," to stop what he called the opposition's terrorism.

On April 19, 2017, opposition supporters organized what they called la madre de todas las marchas, the mother of all marches, with demonstrators turning out in at least a dozen cities across the country at once. In Caracas, marchers trying to reach the city center were met by the National Guard and pro-government colectivos with tear gas and rubber bullets. Multiple people were killed and many more injured that day alone, the opening act of a protest wave that would run for months through the rest of 2017.

The march didn't force Maduro out, and the court's ruling on the Assembly was never really reversed in practice. What it did was mark, publicly and internationally, the moment the standoff between the government and the opposition coalition stopped being a political disagreement and became something closer to open confrontation, the same year the United States began sanctioning Venezuela's financial system. Domestic and international pressure started building at the same time, and neither one let up again for the rest of the decade documented in the next chapter.

## 8. 2005–2025: The Unraveling

Two decades, several threads tightening at once:

**The money.** CADIVI (2003) gives way to SICAD and SICAD II (2013), then CENCOEX, then SIMADI (2015): a rotating cast of exchange-control regimes that never close the gap between the official rate and the street rate. From 2016 through 2020, the bolívar experiences 38 cumulative months of hyperinflation. In August 2018, the currency is redenominated: 100,000 old bolívares fuertes become 1 bolívar soberano. By 2019, the government quietly abandons the fight and the economy becomes unofficially dollarized.

**The succession.** Chávez dies in March 2013. Nicolás Maduro takes over.

**The pressure.** The US begins sanctioning Venezuela's financial system in 2017, then its asset base in 2018, then PDVSA directly in January 2019, then oil shipping. Years of escalation and partial relief follow: a Chevron license in 2022, a wind-down in 2025, expanded licenses for BP, Chevron, Eni, Repsol, and Shell by early 2026.

**The people.** The exodus didn't start with the general economic collapse. It started with a deliberate purge. During the December 2002–February 2003 general strike, an attempt to force Chávez into new elections, the government fired more than 18,000 PDVSA employees for being politically opposed: engineers, geologists, managers, and the highly specialized technical staff who actually knew how to run the country's oil industry. Many of them left for ExxonMobil, Chevron, and other multinationals abroad. It's cited directly as the beginning of Venezuela's brain drain, and PDVSA's operational and maintenance capacity never fully recovered from losing them.

That initial professional exodus was compounded, over the following decade, by a second, distinct pressure: expropriation. From 1999 onward the government nationalized broad swaths of the economy, often without compensation, sometimes announced live on television. Only 15 companies were seized between 2002 and 2006; from 2007 on, that number jumped to 1,147, for a total of 1,162 companies nationalized by mid-2012, plus millions of hectares of farmland. It wasn't only businesses: Chávez personally ordered the expropriation of six residential complexes and the "temporary occupation" of eight gated communities in Caracas and other cities. For anyone who owned a business or a building, staying carried a real, demonstrated risk of simply losing it, which is exactly why a number of Venezuelan companies relocated wholesale to Colombia, Panama, and the United States rather than wait to find out if they were next.

The broader, mass-scale emigration didn't ramp up until the mid-2010s, as the deeper economic collapse set in: national poverty jumped from 48.4% in 2014 to 87% by 2017. Today, more than 7.6 million Venezuelans have been displaced, one of the largest displacement crises anywhere in the world, and for most of them, leaving has not been safe or easy. Hundreds of thousands went on foot through the Darién Gap, a roadless, 60-mile stretch of jungle between Colombia and Panama: fewer than 3,000 Venezuelans crossed it in the entire decade between 2010 and 2021 combined; in 2022 alone, more than 150,000 did. Migrants on this route, known as "caminantes," or walkers, are routinely extorted by the criminal groups controlling it, and robbery and sexual assault are common enough to be treated as a predictable cost of the crossing rather than an aberration. At least 141 deaths were recorded in the Darién in 2023 alone, and the true number is almost certainly higher in terrain this remote and this poorly tracked. Those who survive the jungle and reach other countries in the region often find xenophobia and no path to legal status waiting for them, which is part of why so many kept moving north instead of stopping. Doctors, engineers, teachers: precisely the people this country would need most for a recovery like this one were among those who left, and for millions the departure itself was brutal.

**The institutions.** FUNVISIS, which once ran 250–300 seismic monitoring stations nationwide, is down to 3–5 functioning. Civil protection and fire services get 8–10% of the budget they'd need to actually do their jobs.

**The theft.** Gran Misión Vivienda Venezuela launches in 2011; Transparencia Venezuela's audit finds over $15.5 billion spent with no meaningful oversight. Alex Saab and Álvaro Pulido launder roughly $350 million through fraudulent GMVV housing contracts: a real US criminal conviction, not an allegation. Odebrecht's Venezuela bribery scheme leaves 2,400 unfinished homes in Mariches, Miranda. Diosdado Cabello, OFAC-sanctioned and DOJ-indicted in the same case as Maduro, runs cocaine through the military as part of the so-called Cartel de los Soles.

**The vote.** In 2023, María Corina Machado won the opposition's primary by a landslide to become its unity candidate for the 2024 presidential election. The regime's courts disqualified her from holding office for 15 years over administrative and fiscal violations dating to her time as a legislator, a technicality aimed, transparently, at the government's most dangerous opponent. Her first replacement candidate, Corina Yoris, was blocked from registering too; the regime allowed her second choice, a retired diplomat named Edmundo González Urrutia, to run instead, evidently expecting him to lose quietly.

On July 28, 2024, Venezuela voted. The National Electoral Council declared Maduro the winner, 51.2% to 44.2%, without ever publishing the precinct-level tallies Venezuelan law requires; some polling centers refused outright to print them. The opposition's own poll watchers collected physical tally sheets, known as actas, from over 81% of voting centers nationwide; those showed González winning 67% to Maduro's 30%. The Washington Post independently examined hundreds of the physical tallies, tracked down people whose signatures were on them, and found them authentic. A separate, preregistered statistical exit-poll project sampling nearly a thousand polling stations found almost the identical result: 66.1% to 31.4%. The New York Times documented voter intimidation by state forces and 17,000 voters who found their assigned polling place changed without warning at the last minute. Maduro's government called the opposition's tallies fraudulent and alleged unspecified cyberattacks, but never completed a public audit of its own.

González eventually fled into exile. Machado stayed in Venezuela, in hiding, continuing to insist publicly that González was the country's legitimately elected president. In October 2025 she was awarded the Nobel Peace Prize for her work advancing democratic rights in Venezuela, and couldn't safely leave the country to accept it in person two months later; her daughter stood in for her in Oslo. That's the standing she brings, months later, to her refusal to recognize Delcy Rodríguez's government after Maduro's capture, insisting González, not Rodríguez, is Venezuela's rightful president.

By the end of this stretch, the state has less money, fewer of the people who'd know what to do with it, a hollowed-out emergency-response apparatus, a contested claim to even being a legitimate government, and a well-documented, decades-long habit of treating disaster-response infrastructure as something to loot rather than build.

## 9. 2026: Everything At Once

Six months before the earthquake, Maduro himself was already gone. On January 3, 2026, a US military operation captured him and flew him to New York, where he's now indicted on narco-terrorism charges. His vice president, Delcy Rodríguez, was sworn in as acting president two days later, constitutionally obligated to call an election within 30 days. Five months on, she still hadn't ("I don't know, someday"), the same military and PSUV apparatus remained intact around her, the opposition (María Corina Machado, 2025 Nobel Peace Prize) disputed her legitimacy outright, and Western oil majors resumed operating under expanded US licenses. Analysts called it what it looked like: not reform, but the same regime under new management, with a new foreign patron instead of a confrontation.

That's the government that was in charge when the ground moved again on June 24, 2026: twin earthquakes, magnitude 7.2 and 7.5, 39 seconds apart. FUNVISIS had 3 to 5 working stations left to detect it. In parts of La Guaira, up to 80% of buildings were damaged. Catia La Mar, Caraballeda, Morón, Naiguatá, Caracas, and two areas nobody had even assessed before, El Junquito and Carayaca, all confirmed damaged. Simón Bolívar International Airport's runways cracked, its terminal and control tower damaged badly enough that Venezuela's main gateway to the world closed outright, humanitarian flights rerouted to a military airbase in Maracay. The only road left between Caracas and La Guaira is now clogged with the volunteer traffic the government itself says is "obstructing" the people who are actually supposed to be doing the rescuing.

Soldiers and police were caught looting the rubble on camera; a handful were arrested, most weren't. A German medical team was denied entry. Colombian firefighters were held for hours at the airport. A Chilean rescue team was stopped mid-dig to have its paperwork checked. The opposition's own donation drive was blocked by the National Police. Asked about the criticism, Rodríguez called it "narratives manufactured in propaganda laboratories." PBS didn't mince words either: a "completely ineffective" government, worsening its own earthquake.

None of this is incompetence in isolation: no money, no engineers, no institutions, a government that protects itself before it protects the public, all of it thirty years in the making before this earthquake ever hit. When you understand the last few decades, you understand exactly what the next ten years of recovery are actually up against.

## 10. 2026: Block by Block

Everything in the last chapter happened at once, but it didn't happen the same way everywhere. "The earthquake" is really dozens of different, distinct events depending on which neighborhood you're standing in, and each one explains a different reason this recovery is going to be hard. Four of them, in particular, show the range.

**Same buildings, same warning.** In Los Palos Grandes and neighboring Bello Campo, three towers came down: Petunia 1, Obelisco, and Don Pepe. Petunia 1, fourteen stories, collapsed floor by floor into what rescue workers described as a concrete accordion. Seventeen to eighteen people were pulled out alive; 35 bodies were later recovered from the wreckage. In nearby Altamira, five people were rescued alive from the Edificio Olimpo and sixteen more from a building next door. Engineers looking at the damage afterward pointed to a specific, identifiable flaw: soft-story construction, ground floors built open for parking or retail space with fewer structural columns than the floors above, which collapse first and take everything above them down with it. Venezuela revised its seismic building code after the 1967 earthquake. Enforcement of that code was never made a real priority. Fifty-nine years later, in the same two neighborhoods that lost towers the first time, buildings failed the same way again.

**Never assessed before.** El Junquito and Carayaca, both on the western edge of greater Caracas, were confirmed damaged in the days after the earthquake, but neither had ever had a formal risk assessment before June 24. In Carayaca, a mixed-use residential building collapsed and killed 11 people. There was no existing map of which buildings there were vulnerable and which weren't, no baseline anyone could compare the damage against. Responders were building that picture from scratch, after the fact, in the middle of the emergency, rather than working from something that already existed.

**Almost entirely gone.** Across La Guaira state as a whole, up to 80% of buildings were damaged. In Catia La Mar specifically, a damage assessment from Microsoft's AI for Good Lab estimated that nearly a third of the town's roughly 30,000 buildings were damaged, one of the highest damage rates recorded anywhere in the disaster zone. This isn't a handful of buildings that failed for an identifiable structural reason. It's a town where recovery means, functionally, rebuilding a third of everything.

**Cut off from itself.** Being inside the hardest-hit state doesn't mean every part of it can reach every other part. On June 26, an aftershock collapsed a bridge connecting Caraballeda parish to the rest of La Guaira, disrupting relief work that was already underway there. Zoom out and the whole state has exactly one road connecting it to Caracas. Zoom back in and a single bridge failure inside that state can cut a parish off twice over: once from the rest of the country, and again from the rest of its own state.

None of these four places need the same thing. A fourteen-story tower with an identified structural flaw needs engineers and enforcement. A neighborhood with no prior risk data needs an assessment that should have existed years ago. A town that lost a third of its buildings needs a rebuild at a scale closer to a resettlement than a repair. A parish cut off by a single bridge needs that bridge, specifically, before anything else is even reachable. Recovery isn't one word for one country. It's a different problem in every one of these places, and the response has to be able to tell the difference.

## 11. What Now

Everything so far is history. This last part is a forecast, built only from what's already documented: who got hit hardest, what the response is missing, what happens if the government now in charge doesn't change, and what a realistic decade looks like for a country that walked into this earthquake with the seismic vulnerability of Turkey and the state capacity of Haiti.

**Where it hit hardest.** By ACAPS's July 3 assessment, Distrito Capital and La Guaira took the worst damage of any states and were the first declared disaster zones, followed by Carabobo, Yaracuy, Miranda, Aragua, and Falcón in that order. An estimated 6.6 million people felt the quake: roughly 4.33 million were within the zone where structural damage begins, 690,000 in the zone where damage becomes moderate to significant, and 124,000 in the zone of severe damage. Within Caracas, the Interior Ministry itself named Los Palos Grandes and Altamira, the same neighborhoods where four towers came down in 1967, among the worst-hit areas of the city. In La Guaira, up to 80% of buildings were damaged or destroyed, and up to 848 health facilities across the affected states were confirmed or suspected damaged, including 38 hospitals specifically confirmed hit, at least 20 of which faced severe shortages of medical supplies. None of this damage fell evenly. An estimated 40 to 50% of Caracas's roughly five million residents live in self-built barrio housing on the hillsides ringing the city: unreinforced cinderblock, no formal foundations, no steel reinforcement, built on slopes that were never engineered for anything, let alone a 7.5. Those neighborhoods were the most vulnerable going into June 24, and they're the ones least likely to show up in a formal, fundable reconstruction plan afterward.

**What's missing.** As of the second week of July, somewhere between roughly 16,700 and 17,300 people remained displaced across 79 to 87 temporary shelters, with La Guaira alone housing more than 10,000 people in 26 of them. Aid workers on the ground reported shortages of drinking water, inadequate sanitation, a lack of hygiene and menstrual supplies, and a rising need for psychosocial support nobody was resourced to meet. Hospitals were running out of donated medicine, with families told to buy their own; in the worst-hit areas, there was roughly one healthcare worker for every 1,700 people. On July 8, the UN launched a $296 million appeal to reach 1.3 million people over six months; by the UN's own accounting, that appeal sat against a broader funding gap of roughly $627 million once the full scope of need was counted. This isn't a resourcing gap the world failed to notice, it's one the world noticed and still hasn't closed.

**If the government stays the same.** The reconstruction effort is already being run by people the US Treasury has sanctioned for exactly the kind of behavior this story has been documenting since chapter 6. Diosdado Cabello, Minister of the Interior and the same man with a $25 million US bounty on his head for cocaine trafficking, stood next to Delcy Rodríguez as she announced the government's earthquake response plan, and was separately caught on video blocking a truck of American rescue workers. Rodríguez appointed Rafael Lacava, a state governor already sanctioned for blocking humanitarian aid deliveries while moving corrupt proceeds into Swiss and Andorran accounts, to her reconstruction commission. Six of the senior officials now leading the response are under US sanction for corruption, money laundering, drug trafficking, or repression. A German medical team was denied entry. Colombian firefighters were held for hours at the airport. A Chilean rescue team was stopped mid-dig to have its paperwork checked. If the people running this reconstruction are the same people who spent the last decade running Gran Misión Vivienda Venezuela and the Cartel de los Soles, there's no documented reason to expect the money behaves differently this time.

**The next ten years.** Two other earthquakes offer the closest real comparisons, and Venezuela is positioned to inherit the worst of both. Haiti's 2010 earthquake drew $13.5 to $16.3 billion in pledges; of what was actually disbursed, Haitian organizations and firms received only about 2% directly, with the rest routed through international NGOs and private contractors whose own accounting was thin enough that nobody can say with confidence where most of it went (the American Red Cross alone raised $500 million for Haiti and, per a widely cited NPR/ProPublica investigation, built six houses). Turkey's 2023 earthquake killed nearly 46,000 people in Turkey alone, in a country with functioning institutions, in large part because a 2018 construction amnesty let more than 10 million applicants pay a fee to legalize buildings that skipped earthquake-safety codes; after Turkey's 1999 quake, a similar wave of 2,100 lawsuits against contractors ended in 1,800 criminal amnesties and a single imprisonment. Venezuela now carries Turkey's problem (decades of unregulated, code-skipping construction on unstable ground) without Turkey's functioning state, and Haiti's problem (billions of aid dollars flowing through channels nobody can fully audit) without Haiti's comparatively cooperative government. Watch, over the next decade, for whether reconstruction contracts go to the same sanctioned names already sitting on the reconstruction commission, whether the barrios get rebuilt to any different standard than the ones that just came down, whether FUNVISIS (down to 3 to 5 working seismic stations from a former 250 to 300) ever gets meaningfully rebuilt, and whether the engineers and technical staff this country has been losing since the 2002 PDVSA strike have anywhere to come back to.

**What's actually possible.** The emergency phase (water, shelter, trauma care) is a solvable problem with enough sustained funding and actual humanitarian access, and the aid pledged so far (over $300 million from the US, the UN's $296 million appeal, Venezuela's own $4.85 billion IMF request against reconstruction costs UNDRR puts at roughly $37 billion) is at least the right order of magnitude to start. What isn't realistic, based on everything documented in this history, is a clean, fast, corruption-free national rebuild. Neither Haiti nor Turkey achieved that with far more functioning governments and far more international goodwill, and Venezuela is attempting it with a reconstruction commission that already includes sanctioned officials, a government that spent the earthquake's first weeks blocking rescue workers, and thirty years of practice treating disaster-response money as something to divert rather than spend. The realistic version of the next ten years looks like a two-track recovery: a visible, diaspora- and NGO-driven rebuilding effort in a handful of neighborhoods people are watching closely, running alongside a much larger, slower, heavily skimmed formal process, in which a meaningful share of whatever gets unlocked, whether from Caracas, Washington, or the IMF, ends up somewhere other than the people who lost their homes.

## 12. Voices from the Ground

Everything above is analysis: numbers, patterns, institutions. None of it happened to a statistic. It happened to specific people, in specific buildings, on one morning in June, and the parts of this story that are hardest to hold are the ones that are hardest to summarize. So instead of summarizing them, here are some of them, in their own words, as reported by the journalists who were there.

**The morning it happened.** Tony Frangie, a journalist, was in a Caracas elevator when the first quake hit. "I started to pray and to press every button, waiting for it to open up," he told CBS Mornings. The elevator let him out in the basement; the building stayed standing, and he spent the next several hours waiting outside with neighbors as the news started to reach him. "We've seen videos of buildings collapsing and never-ending amount of people looking for families," he said. "So, yeah, we expect it to get worse." In La Guaira, Antonio Bermudez was in his living room when the shaking began "all of a sudden." "I started to move, I looked for shelter under a column. I was between my room and the shower. It shook harder and harder," he told AFP. "I held onto the wall and the building started to come down."

**Digging out.** In Catia La Mar, a neighborhood of nearly 200 housing towers, Yilsmaris Blanco, 39, told AFP "everything, everything collapsed." "We thank God because we're alive, but there are people right now suffering with their relatives buried, with their relatives crushed and they can't get them out," she said. Larry Rojas, 49, had family trapped in a collapsed building nearby. "We have nothing, right now we have nothing, not even the strength or the courage to go in there," he told AFP. People had no access to water, he said, leaving them "dying of thirst." "Really, we need someone to help us, to send machinery. That's what we need to get into the buildings that have collapsed," he said, adding that residents were now afraid to enter even the buildings still standing, because "we're afraid it will collapse too."

**Eight days under a shopping center.** Hernán Alberto Gil Flores, a 43-year-old night-shift security guard, was trapped for eight days in the collapsed basement of the Galerías Playa Grande shopping center in Catia La Mar, kept alive past the 48-to-72-hour survival window rescuers usually plan around because his small security cabin held its shape while the concrete around it came down. Costa Rican Red Cross rescuer Minyar Collado, part of the team that first made contact with him, told the AP: "When we found him, he asked us not to tell his wife that he was alive, just in case he wouldn't make it." "We were never going to leave him here," she said. His wife, Gusbimar González, told the AP she had spent days in despair before hearing rescuers had reached him. "When I learned he was alive, I saw a ray of light in the darkness," she said. During the final hours of the extraction, Chilean firefighter María Paz Campos talked him through each step over a video line, telling him at one point, "I need you to keep the goggles on, for the small particles that are falling, to avoid them getting into your eye." He was pulled out alive on July 2.

**What's left to treat.** The physical rescue is only part of what a disaster like this leaves behind. "We've already gone through a period of complex trauma, which will continue to occur, but now it's complicated by infections," Eugenio Cova, head of the trauma unit at Hospital José Gregorio Hernández in Caracas, told Al Jazeera and the AP. Kevin Simm, a volunteer aid worker, described the scale of destruction in terms usually reserved for war reporting: "This obviously brings to mind the current situations that are going on across Gaza and Ukraine. It's like a scene from a movie or from a war zone. We have never seen this in peacetime." Huniades Urbina, of the board of Venezuela's pediatrics association, put the health system's failure plainly: the earthquakes, he said, "once again highlight the Venezuelan government's inability to provide an adequate healthcare system that meets the needs of the Venezuelan people." David Smilde, a Tulane University researcher who has studied Venezuela for decades, put it more bluntly to The World/PRX: "They've had the capacity to repress people and jail people, but they don't seem to be in a position to offer even some of the most basic elements of disaster relief."

**Anger, on camera.** Some of this anger was captured live, by people filming it as it happened. In La Guaira, a civilian rescue worker confronted soldiers standing near the rubble with rifles instead of tools: "Why did you bring weapons? You should have brought a shovel, a pickaxe. We're not at war. We're in an emergency," he said, in a video widely shared and reported by The World/PRX. In one of the most widely circulated clips of the response, residents at the Vallarta residences in Playa Grande surrounded a CICPC officer accused of pocketing cash pulled from the rubble instead of helping search it, tearing the bills up in front of him on camera, a video posted by journalist Patricia Janiot that went viral. Opposition leader Juan Pablo Guanipa posted his own condemnation of the military's conduct during the response: "The videos of soldiers, under orders from the corrupt Military High Command, confiscating or preventing the entry of aid collected by our people are unacceptable." When acting President Delcy Rodríguez visited the ruins of the collapsed Petunia tower in Chacao on June 26, relatives of people still buried underneath did not hold back, shouting: "Enough with the political campaign amid a tragedy like the one we're living through."

None of these people are speaking for the whole country. They're each describing one morning, one building, one ward, from wherever they happened to be standing in it. That's the point of putting them here rather than folding them into the numbers above: the numbers explain the shape of the disaster, but these are what it actually felt like from inside it.
