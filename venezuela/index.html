Putting the 2026 Caracas earthquakes in context

This isn't a risk map. It's a risk history. And maybe a risk future.

The devastating earthquakes that hit Venezuela two weeks ago did not happen in a vacuum. To understand why the damage and the response is so damning, it's important to understand how we got here, from the beginning. From past earthquakes and landslides to attempted coups to the gutting of public infrastructure to the biggest brain-drain and outmigration in modern history. The disaster started a long time ago, and it's not done yet.

Chapter 0 — 1812: M7.7

"Si la naturaleza se opone, lucharemos contra ella y haremos que nos obedezca."

It would be convenient to say Venezuela simply didn't know this could happen, that geology caught a country unprepared to even imagine this, that rotting infrastructure was for lack of need, or knowledge, or understanding.

Except it's not true, and it's never been. This is one of the founding stories of the country, the kind every Venezuelan child learns in school: on March 26, 1812, a magnitude-7.7 earthquake tore through Caracas, killing some 15,000 people and destroying the city almost entirely. The ground shook hard enough to be felt in Bogotá, more than 700 kilometers away.

Simón Bolívar was 29 when it hit, back in Caracas from his military campaigns for independence, a Spanish invasion on the horizon. Witnesses described him in a tattered, stained shirt, sleeves rolled up, pulling bodies out of the rubble with his own hands. People called it God's punishment for the "sin of independence." Bolívar argued with them instead of humoring them. At one point he drew his sword on a priest who was praying over the dead instead of digging for the living, and ordered him to start pulling bodies out instead. Two centuries before soldiers would be filmed looting earthquake rubble instead of clearing it, this was the opposite instinct: survivors first, everything else later.

A royalist doctor found him standing on a pile of ruins and asked, mockingly, what he had to say now that nature itself had turned against the republic. Bolívar's answer is one of the most quoted lines in Venezuelan history: "Si la naturaleza se opone, lucharemos contra ella y haremos que nos obedezca." If nature opposes us, we will fight it and make it obey.

Earthquakes were part of Venezuela's beginning. Whether it happens again is the rest of this story.

Chapter 1 — 1967: m6.6

The first earthquake of the modern era.

On July 29, 1967, a magnitude 6.5–6.6 earthquake hit the Venezuelan coast about 20 kilometers from Caracas. Four apartment towers in Altamira and Los Palos Grandes (Edificio San José, Mijagual, Neverí, and Palace/Corvin) collapsed completely. Mansión Charaima and the Hotel Macuto Sheraton in Caraballeda and Macuto lost most of their floors. Two hundred to 236 people died, about 2,000 were injured, 40 buildings were declared uninhabitable, 180 more were severely damaged.

This is not a story about corruption. Not yet, at least.

In 1967, Venezuela was a functioning democracy, oil-wealthy, thirty-one years from Chávez, with none of the notorious, weaponized cronyism that defines the later chapters of this story. The ground did this anyway. That's the point of starting here: the seismic risk under Caracas and the Vargas coast isn't a symptom of anything that came later. It's older than all of it, and it was never going away. What changes across the next fifty-nine years isn't the risk. It's the country's capacity to answer it.

Chapter 1½ — 1967–1982: writing the rules after the fact

Provisional, permanently.

Within months of the 1967 earthquake, Venezuela had a new seismic code on paper. The Ministry of Public Works issued the Norma Provisional para Construcciones Antisísmicas that same year, replacing 1939-era building rules that hadn't accounted for earthquakes at all. It was provisional by name, and it stayed that way for a decade and a half.

In November 1971, four years after the quake, the government created FUNVISIS, giving Venezuela its first dedicated body for seismological research and monitoring. On paper, it was exactly the long-horizon institution an earthquake country needs, something that keeps working on hazard whether or not politicians are paying attention that year.

For most of the golden years covered in the next chapter, that's largely what FUNVISIS did: research, much of it good, running mostly in the background while the oil money went toward scholarships, orchestras, and steel mills. Turning that research into an actual, enforceable update to the 1967 provisional code took until 1982, when the country finally adopted its first fully modern seismic-resistant building standard.

The new code landed one year before the 1983 currency devaluation that hastened the country's economic freefall. Between the earthquake that justified it and the code that finally answered it, an entire generation of Caracas kept getting built up under building standards everyone in the field already knew were barely provisional.

Nearly six decades later, when the 2026 earthquakes hit the same fault lines, investigators found the same basic pattern repeating: many of the structures that collapsed or were severely damaged had gone up in the periods before whichever seismic code applied to them, or simply hadn't been built to code at all. A seismic code, it turned out, was never a one-time fix. It was upkeep the country kept deferring, decade after decade, long after 1982.

Chapter 2 — the boom years

Wealth without a floor.

"Ta' barato, dame dos."

By 1950, Venezuela had the fourth-highest GDP per capita on Earth, twice as rich as Chile, four times richer than Japan, twelve times richer than China. It stayed the richest major economy in Latin America into the 1980s. The 1970s OPEC oil shock quadrupled its revenues; between 1974 and 1979 the government spent more, in absolute terms, than in the entirety of its prior independent history.

None of that wealth ever had to prove it could survive contact with a real crisis. A country that rich, on a single commodity, doesn't need deep institutions: it needs a functioning oil ministry and enough cash to paper over everything else.

Some of the oil money built real things: schools, hospitals, roads, agricultural subsidies. But a lot of it went somewhere else. Under President Jaime Lusinchi in the mid-1980s, a currency-exchange program called RECADI was supposed to hand out cheap, government-subsidized dollars to importers. Instead, well-connected buyers purchased those dollars at the artificial official rate and resold them on the real market for enormous profit; the Cato Institute later estimated $36 billion moved through the scheme. This wasn't a one-time mistake so much as a design flaw the country would keep rebuilding.

The people who benefited from schemes like RECADI weren't a single, easily labeled group. Some of the same people who spent later decades in the opposition made real money off currency arbitrage like this themselves, knowingly contributing to the country's decay. That isn't necessarily hypocrisy on its own. For most of this era, proximity to state contracts and preferential exchange rates was one of the only reliable paths to a stable living in Venezuela, and plenty of people ended up inside that system by necessity rather than conviction. The same arrangement that enriched insiders also left almost anyone who wanted an ordinary life with few ways to build one that didn't run through it somehow.

Then, in the mid-1980s, a global oil glut cut prices by roughly 70% in under a year. An economy that had never diversified had nothing left to fall back on.

Chapter 2½ — 1974–1983: the golden years

Sowing the oil.

It's important to give credit where credit is due. The good times were really good, and there was a window when the future looked exceptionally bright. But Icarus' fall was just as spectacular as his flight.

The 1973 OPEC oil shock quadrupled Venezuela's export revenue almost overnight, and Carlos Andrés Pérez took office in March 1974 promising to spend it differently this time. Historians would later describe his program, eventually codified as the V Plan de la Nación (1976–1980), as a revival of an old idea: writer and diplomat Arturo Uslar Pietri had argued back in 1936 that a country living off a resource it would someday run out of needed to sembrar el petróleo, to sow the oil, and plant it into something that could outlast it.

Some of that investment was real, and some of it transformed lives. In June 1974, the government created the Fundación Gran Mariscal de Ayacucho, a scholarship program to send Venezuelans abroad to study engineering, petroleum science, and the technical fields the country's newly nationalized oil and steel industries would need. The first cohort, 1,000 students, left that September. By 1978, the program had met its original target of roughly 15,000 scholars sent abroad. The scholarships alone didn't last, though: after 1978 the foundation leaned increasingly on educational credits instead of outright grants, and by the 1990s those credits carried interest rates as high as 58%, a far cry from what the first cohort had signed up for.

In February 1975, economist and musician José Antonio Abreu held the first rehearsal of what would become El Sistema, in an underground parking garage in Caracas. Eleven musicians showed up. Within ten weeks there were eighty. Pérez's government began funding it directly, and in 1979 it was formalized as a state foundation. Decades later its graduates would include Gustavo Dudamel and Edicson Ruiz, sent onto the world's most important stages by a program built on the same oil money as everything else in this chapter.

On January 1, 1976, Pérez nationalized the oil industry outright, creating PDVSA, and the state used its new control over the country's core export to fund the rest of the plan: steel production at SIDOR, aluminum output at Venalum that nearly tripled by 1978, a second stage at the Guri dam, and a new Litoral campus for the Universidad Simón Bolívar (founded a few years earlier, in 1967) that opened in 1977. For a few years, it was a massive, ambitious attempt to convert a commodity windfall into a diversified, educated, self-sufficient country.

But the same money that built universities, orchestras, and steel mills also moved through channels nobody ever fully accounted for. Between 1977 and 1980, the state's development corporation paid a Norwegian shipping company $20 million for a refrigerated cargo vessel independently appraised at closer to $12 million; a congressional investigation later found Pérez himself politically responsible, in a vote of 132 to 102. A wider circle of businessmen who had financed his 1973 campaign, later nicknamed the "twelve apostles," found themselves first in line for state contracts throughout the same years. Nothing here was a single fortune skimmed the way later scandals would be. It was something quieter: a state building remarkable things while its own money kept leaking out of the same pipe that was funding them.

Luis Alberto Machado had already published La Revolución de la Inteligencia in 1975, in the middle of the boom, arguing that intelligence itself, not just infrastructure, could be built at scale through state-funded education. In March 1979, Pérez's successor made him the world's first Minister for the Development of Intelligence.

That October, his ministry and Abreu's orchestra program staged a demonstration meant to prove the point: Pemón children from the Gran Sabana, most of whom had never touched a violin, learned the instrument well enough in about two months to perform the finale of Beethoven's Ninth at the Teatro Teresa Carreño, with President Herrera Campins in the audience. It was, in its way, the high-water mark of the same optimism that had funded the scholarships and the orchestras a few years earlier: the belief that a government could still engineer a smarter, more capable country faster than its economy could fall apart.

Not even four years later, on February 18, 1983, a day Venezuelans still call Viernes Negro, falling oil prices and a debt load the country could no longer service forced the first currency devaluation in a generation.

For everyone the boom clearly reached, there was another Venezuela the ledger never quite covered. Caracas's barrios kept growing right through the years of Ayacucho scholars and steel mills, roughly a fifth larger by 1981 than they had been a decade earlier, and they still held close to the same two-fifths share of the city's population they always had. The boom hadn't shrunk the informal city so much as grown up around it. When the reckoning came, it came fast: poverty in Venezuela nearly doubled in just four years, from under 18% in 1980 to more than 32% by 1983. Whatever the golden years were for the people they were built for, for a large share of the country they were mostly just the years right before everything got dramatically worse.

Not everyone got a redemption arc. Thousands of Ayacucho scholars finished their engineering and science degrees abroad in the early 1980s, right as the country that was supposed to need them started contracting instead of growing. Some came home to jobs that no longer existed the way they'd been promised. Many more didn't come home at all, an early wave of the brain drain that would only deepen in the decades after. The swing up had been brief and beautiful. The swing back down was already underway before most of the people it was built for ever got the chance to use what they'd learned.

Chapter 3 — 1989–1992: the cracks begin to show

A riot, two coups, and a man with a promise.

The golden years never dealt with the inequality riding alongside them, and that unaddressed gap is part of why the country had nothing left to fall back on once the money stopped.

On February 27, 1989, President Carlos Andrés Pérez announced an austerity package meant to address the debt the boom years had left behind, among the changes a 30% overnight hike in public transport fares. Riots and looting broke out in Guarenas and spread to Caracas within a day. The government declared a curfew and activated Plan Ávila, putting the capital under martial law and soldiers in the streets with live ammunition. The official death toll was 276; other estimates run as high as 3,000. Venezuelans call it the Caracazo.

Three years later, on February 4, 1992, army units led by a mid-ranking officer named Hugo Chávez tried to seize Miraflores Palace in a coup attempt against Pérez. It failed, and Chávez went to prison, but the attempt made him a national figure anyway. A second coup attempt followed on November 27, 1992. It was also put down, at a far higher cost: 172 dead.

By 1998, when Chávez ran for president as a civilian, he wasn't an outsider promising something new out of nowhere. He was the face of a rebellion against a government that had already shot its own citizens in the street over a bus fare.

Chapter 4 — 1998: hugo chávez

The rise of Chavismo.

Hugo Chávez was elected president in December 1998. This chapter is deliberately short, because 1998 itself isn't the catastrophe: it's the pivot after which everything else in this story happens under one consolidating political project, for better and for worse.

Chapter 5 — 1999: a different kind of disaster

Vargas.

Fourteen months after Chávez took office, on December 14–16, 1999, extreme rainfall (up to 911mm over a few days) triggered debris flows along roughly 100 kilometers of the Vargas coast, burying entire neighborhoods between Catia La Mar and past Caruao. It wasn't the country's first warning: the 1967 earthquake, thirty-two years earlier and on the same stretch of coast, had already shown what weak construction and thin state capacity meant for this land.

Whole neighborhoods disappeared under the debris: Los Corales was buried under roughly three meters of hardened mud and rock, and towns like Carmen de Uria and Cerro Grande were wiped off the map entirely. The material didn't wash away, it set almost like concrete, on the same alluvial fans that had carried debris flows down from the mountains for thousands of years. Within a decade, new construction had gone up on top of it, because on a coastline this steep, the fan is often the only flat ground there is.

The response gave a preview of habits that would recur for decades: the government's attention that week was on a referendum for Chávez's new constitution, scheduled for December 15, the same day the rain turned into landslides, and no evacuation was organized before the worst of it hit.

In the weeks after, two US Navy ships offering personnel and equipment, the USS Nashville and USS Tortuga, were initially turned away, even as Chávez separately accepted a US Army emergency team on the ground, and security forces sent in to stop looting were accused of abuses of their own.

The official death toll is estimated between 700 and 3,000, and Vargas state's population fell by roughly 77,700 between 1999 and 2000. The hardest-hit parishes, Caraballeda, Macuto, and Naiguatá, were also where informal, undocumented housing was thickest, built on the same debris fans the mountains had used before. That housing had never been reliably counted in the first place, part of why the disaster hit as hard as it did (no one had a clear picture of how many people were living in harm's way), and part of why recovery took so long afterward, since there was no real baseline to resettle people against or account for the missing.

This isn't a story about the rain being anyone's fault. It's the first real stress test, in a generation, of whatever disaster-response capacity Venezuela still had.

Chapter 6 — 2005: the second time

Rain, mud, and imaginary money.

In February 2005, it happened again. The Pacairigua river and Silma stream overflowed in Guatire; the Guaire river overflowed through Caracas during a record 84.7mm rainfall in 24 hours. Seven states were placed under national emergency.

The damage was concentrated in specific places: a dam in Miranda state breached and sent a wall of water and mud that leveled the town of Nuevo Guapo almost entirely, the overflowing Capayita and Araira rivers destroyed the nearby town of Araira, and in Guatire, the Valle Arriba neighborhood was buried under mud that swept away or ruined most of its homes.

2005 was really two disasters happening at once. One was the mud and the rain. The other was economic: the currency-control system created in 2003 (CADIVI) is now two years old, and an arbitrage economy is becoming a way of life for anyone positioned to profit from the gap. Venezuelans already had a word for it: chanchullo, a rigged deal, a hustle run through the state rather than around it.

It took hold so fast because the incentives ran only one way: CADIVI's official rate sat far below what dollars actually traded for, and access to it depended less on need than on who you knew inside the ministry signing off. Shell import companies collected subsidized dollars for goods that were never shipped; officials and their proxies ran the same play with the state's own oil revenue, at a scale ordinary corruption never reached.

Every dollar skimmed that way was a dollar that didn't go into hospitals, roads, or the seismic monitoring network FUNVISIS was already struggling to keep funded. The cracks 2005 exposed weren't only in the hillsides. They were the first visible sign of a state quietly redirecting its own money away from the things that would decide how the next disaster went.

Eventually the bolívar became worthless enough that street vendors started folding it into banknote origami, to sell as souvenirs instead of spending it as money.

Chapter 7 — 2017: the mother of all marches

The law ceases to matter.

On March 29, 2017, Venezuela's Supreme Court, packed with Maduro loyalists, stripped the opposition-controlled National Assembly of its legislative powers and effectively assumed them itself. The National Assembly was the one branch of government the opposition actually held, having won it in a landslide in 2015. The court's move was, functionally, a self-coup: the government dissolving the last national body it didn't control, through a legal ruling instead of a tank.

The response came three weeks later, and the government didn't wait for it quietly. Two days before the march, Maduro ordered the Venezuelan National Militia expanded to 500,000 members, each one promised a rifle, framing it as insurance against a repeat of the 2002 coup attempt against him. Diosdado Cabello, already a senior PSUV official, said 60,000 motorized colectivos and the militia would be spread across Caracas on April 19, "until necessary," to stop what he called the opposition's terrorism.

On April 19, 2017, opposition supporters organized what they called la madre de todas las marchas, the mother of all marches, with demonstrators turning out in at least a dozen cities across the country at once. In Caracas, marchers trying to reach the city center were met by the National Guard and pro-government colectivos with tear gas and rubber bullets. Multiple people were killed and many more injured that day alone, the opening act of a protest wave that would run for months through the rest of 2017.

The march didn't force Maduro out, and the court's ruling on the Assembly was never really reversed in practice. What it did was mark, publicly and internationally, the moment the standoff between the government and the opposition coalition stopped being a political disagreement and became something closer to open confrontation, the same year the United States began sanctioning Venezuela's financial system. Domestic and international pressure started building at the same time, and neither one let up again for the rest of the decade documented in the next chapter.

Chapter 8 — 2005–2025: a short story of total collapse

Everything unravels.

"All who served the revolution have plowed the sea."

Simón Bolívar, 1830, nearing the end of his life, lamenting that his struggle for independence had led to political chaos, instability, and the undoing of his dream for a united Latin America

Two decades, several threads tightening at once:

The money

CADIVI (2003) gives way to SICAD and SICAD II (2013), then CENCOEX, then SIMADI (2015). From 2016 through 2020, the bolívar experiences 38 cumulative months of hyperinflation.

The people

The exodus started with a deliberate purge: during the 2002–2003 general strike, the government fired more than 18,000 PDVSA employees. Today more than 7.6 million Venezuelans have been displaced. Hundreds of thousands went on foot through the Darién Gap.

The institutions

FUNVISIS, which once ran 250–300 seismic monitoring stations nationwide, is down to 3–5 functioning.

The vote

In 2023, María Corina Machado won the opposition's primary by a landslide. On July 28, 2024, the National Electoral Council declared Maduro the winner without ever publishing precinct-level tallies. Machado was awarded the 2025 Nobel Peace Prize.

Chapter 9 — 2026: m7.2 and m7.5, 39 seconds apart

June 24, 2026.

Six months before the earthquake, Maduro himself was already gone. On January 3, 2026, a US military operation captured him and flew him to New York, where he's now indicted on narco-terrorism charges. His vice president, Delcy Rodríguez, was sworn in as acting president two days later, constitutionally obligated to call an election within 30 days. Five months on, she still hadn't, the same military and PSUV apparatus remained intact around her, and the opposition disputed her legitimacy outright.

That's the government that was in charge when the ground moved again on June 24, 2026: twin earthquakes, magnitude 7.2 and 7.5, 39 seconds apart. FUNVISIS had 3 to 5 working stations left to detect it. In parts of La Guaira, up to 80% of buildings were damaged. Catia La Mar, Caraballeda, Morón, Naiguatá, Caracas, El Junquito, and Carayaca all confirmed damaged.

Chapter 10 — 2026: block by block

A view from the ground.

Everything in the last chapter happened at once, but it didn't happen the same way everywhere. "The earthquake" is really dozens of different, distinct events depending on which neighborhood you're standing in, and each one explains a different reason this recovery is going to be hard. Four of them, in particular, show the range.

Same buildings, same warning

In Los Palos Grandes and neighboring Bello Campo, three towers came down: Petunia 1, Obelisco, and Don Pepe. Petunia 1, fourteen stories, collapsed floor by floor into what rescue workers described as a concrete accordion. Seventeen to eighteen people were pulled out alive; 35 bodies were later recovered from the wreckage. In nearby Altamira, five people were rescued alive from the Edificio Olimpo and sixteen more from a building next door. Engineers looking at the damage afterward pointed to a specific, identifiable flaw: soft-story construction, ground floors built open for parking or retail space with fewer structural columns than the floors above, which collapse first and take everything above them down with it. Venezuela revised its seismic building code after the 1967 earthquake. Enforcement of that code was never made a real priority. Fifty-nine years later, in the same two neighborhoods that lost towers the first time, buildings failed the same way again.

Never assessed before

El Junquito and Carayaca, both on the western edge of greater Caracas, were confirmed damaged in the days after the earthquake, but neither had ever had a formal risk assessment before June 24. In Carayaca, a mixed-use residential building collapsed and killed 11 people. There was no existing map of which buildings there were vulnerable and which weren't, no baseline anyone could compare the damage against. Responders were building that picture from scratch, after the fact, in the middle of the emergency, rather than working from something that already existed.

Almost entirely gone

Across La Guaira state as a whole, up to 80% of buildings were damaged. In Catia La Mar specifically, a damage assessment from Microsoft's AI for Good Lab estimated that nearly a third of the town's roughly 30,000 buildings were damaged, one of the highest damage rates recorded anywhere in the disaster zone. This isn't a handful of buildings that failed for an identifiable structural reason. It's a town where recovery means, functionally, rebuilding a third of everything.

Cut off from itself

Being inside the hardest-hit state doesn't mean every part of it can reach every other part. On June 26, an aftershock collapsed a bridge connecting Caraballeda parish to the rest of La Guaira, disrupting relief work that was already underway there. Zoom out and the whole state has exactly one road connecting it to Caracas. Zoom back in and a single bridge failure inside that state can cut a parish off twice over: once from the rest of the country, and again from the rest of its own state.

None of these four places need the same thing. A fourteen-story tower with an identified structural flaw needs engineers and enforcement. A neighborhood with no prior risk data needs an assessment that should have existed years ago. A town that lost a third of its buildings needs a rebuild at a scale closer to a resettlement than a repair. A parish cut off by a single bridge needs that bridge, specifically, before anything else is even reachable. Recovery isn't one word for one country. It's a different problem in every one of these places, and the response has to be able to tell the difference.

Chapter 11 — what now

Reading the next ten years.

Everything so far is history. This last part is a forecast, built only from what's already documented: who got hit hardest, what the response is missing, what happens if the government now in charge doesn't change, and what a realistic decade looks like for a country that walked into this earthquake with the seismic vulnerability of Turkey and the state capacity of Haiti.

Where it hit hardest

Distrito Capital and La Guaira took the worst damage, followed by Carabobo, Yaracuy, Miranda, Aragua, and Falcón. An estimated 40 to 50% of Caracas's roughly five million residents live in self-built barrio housing on hillsides never engineered for a 7.5.

What's missing

Roughly 16,700 to 17,300 people remained displaced across 79 to 87 temporary shelters as of mid-July. On July 8 the UN launched a $296 million appeal against a broader funding gap of roughly $627 million.

If the government stays the same

Diosdado Cabello, with a $25 million US bounty on his head, stood next to Rodríguez announcing the response plan and was separately caught blocking a rescue truck on video. Rafael Lacava sits on the reconstruction commission despite his own sanctions.

What's actually possible

Venezuela now carries Turkey's construction problem without Turkey's functioning state, and Haiti's aid-accountability problem without Haiti's cooperative government. The realistic version of the next ten years looks like a two-track recovery.

Chapter 12 — voices from the ground

What it actually felt like.

Everything above is analysis: numbers, patterns, institutions. None of it happened to a statistic. It happened to specific people, in specific buildings, on one morning in June, and the parts of this story that are hardest to hold are the ones that are hardest to summarize. So instead of summarizing them, here are some of them, in their own words, as reported by the journalists who were there.

The morning it happened

Tony Frangie, a journalist, was in a Caracas elevator when the first quake hit.

"I started to pray and to press every button, waiting for it to open up."

Tony Frangie, to CBS Mornings, June 25, 2026

The elevator let him out in the basement. The building stayed standing, and he spent the next several hours waiting outside with neighbors as the news started to reach him.

"We've seen videos of buildings collapsing and never-ending amount of people looking for families. So, yeah, we expect it to get worse."

Tony Frangie, to CBS Mornings, June 25, 2026

In La Guaira, Antonio Bermudez was in his living room when the shaking began "all of a sudden."

"I started to move, I looked for shelter under a column. I was between my room and the shower. It shook harder and harder. I held onto the wall and the building started to come down."

Antonio Bermudez, to AFP, June 25, 2026

Digging out

In Catia La Mar, a neighborhood of nearly 200 housing towers, Yilsmaris Blanco, 39, described what she came back to.

"Everything, everything collapsed. We thank God because we're alive, but there are people right now suffering with their relatives buried, with their relatives crushed and they can't get them out."

Yilsmaris Blanco, to AFP, June 25, 2026

Larry Rojas, 49, had family trapped in a collapsed building nearby.

"We have nothing, right now we have nothing, not even the strength or the courage to go in there. Really, we need someone to help us, to send machinery. That's what we need to get into the buildings that have collapsed."

Larry Rojas, to AFP, June 25, 2026

People had no access to water, he said, leaving them "dying of thirst," and residents were now afraid to enter even the buildings still standing, because "we're afraid it will collapse too."

Eight days under a shopping center

Hernán Alberto Gil Flores, a 43-year-old night-shift security guard, was trapped for eight days in the collapsed basement of the Galerías Playa Grande shopping center in Catia La Mar, kept alive past the 48-to-72-hour survival window rescuers usually plan around because his small security cabin held its shape while the concrete around it came down.

"When we found him, he asked us not to tell his wife that he was alive, just in case he wouldn't make it. We were never going to leave him here."

Minyar Collado, Costa Rican Red Cross rescuer, to AP, July 2, 2026

His wife, Gusbimar González, had spent days in despair before hearing rescuers had reached him.

"When I learned he was alive, I saw a ray of light in the darkness."

Gusbimar González, to AP, July 2, 2026

During the final hours of the extraction, Chilean firefighter María Paz Campos talked him through each step over a video line.

"I need you to keep the goggles on, for the small particles that are falling, to avoid them getting into your eye."

María Paz Campos, to Hernán Alberto Gil Flores, via AP video, July 2, 2026

He was pulled out alive on July 2.

What's left to treat

The physical rescue is only part of what a disaster like this leaves behind.

"We've already gone through a period of complex trauma, which will continue to occur, but now it's complicated by infections."

Eugenio Cova, head of the trauma unit, Hospital José Gregorio Hernández, Caracas, to Al Jazeera/AP, July 2, 2026

"This obviously brings to mind the current situations that are going on across Gaza and Ukraine. It's like a scene from a movie or from a war zone. We have never seen this in peacetime."

Kevin Simm, volunteer aid worker, to Al Jazeera, July 2, 2026

The earthquakes "once again highlight the Venezuelan government's inability to provide an adequate healthcare system that meets the needs of the Venezuelan people."

Huniades Urbina, board member, Venezuelan Pediatric Society, to Al Jazeera/AP, July 2, 2026

David Smilde, a Tulane University researcher who has studied Venezuela for decades, put the state's failure in blunter terms.

"They've had the capacity to repress people and jail people, but they don't seem to be in a position to offer even some of the most basic elements of disaster relief."

David Smilde, to The World/PRX, July 2, 2026

Weaponized incompetence, or worse

Some of this anger was captured live, by people filming it as it happened. In La Guaira, a civilian rescue worker confronted soldiers standing near the rubble with rifles instead of tools.

"Why did you bring weapons? You should have brought a shovel, a pickaxe. We're not at war. We're in an emergency."

Unnamed rescue worker, La Guaira, reported by The World/PRX, July 2, 2026

In one of the most widely shared videos of the response, residents at the Vallarta residences in Playa Grande surrounded a CICPC officer they accused of pocketing cash pulled from the rubble instead of helping search it, tearing the bills up in front of him on camera. Video, via journalist Patricia Janiot: open direct ↗

Opposition leader Juan Pablo Guanipa posted his own condemnation of the military's conduct during the response.

"The videos of soldiers, under orders from the corrupt Military High Command, confiscating or preventing the entry of aid collected by our people are unacceptable."

Juan Pablo Guanipa, opposition leader, via X/Twitter, July 1, 2026

One of several videos circulating of soldiers and police accused of standing by, extorting residents, or looting instead of helping: open direct ↗

When acting President Delcy Rodríguez visited the ruins of the collapsed Petunia tower in Chacao, relatives of people still buried underneath did not hold back.

"Enough with the political campaign amid a tragedy like the one we're living through."

Resident, shouted at Delcy Rodríguez, Chacao, June 26, 2026

None of these people are speaking for the whole country. They're each describing one morning, one building, one ward, from wherever they happened to be standing in it. That's the point of putting them here rather than folding them into the numbers above: the numbers explain the shape of the disaster, but these are what it actually felt like from inside it.

Everything above is history and analysis. Everything below is live, changing by the hour, and built by people outside any government channel, mostly volunteers, doing accounting the state itself hasn't. None of these are affiliated with this project, and none of them have been independently verified here beyond confirming the sites exist and describe themselves this way.
